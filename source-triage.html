Source Triage

Six things you might find while researching our food-label question. For each one, decide: do you trust it, does it depend, or do you skip it as evidence?

Use SIFT as you go: Stop · Investigate the source (who made this, and why?) · Find better coverage · Trace the claim to where it started. And ask our lens question: whose interest does this serve?

Sort first, reveal later. Make your own calls before you open the verdict. Then we argue them out as a class.

Maybe the Harvard study is real, maybe it isn't. Go find it, open it, read it. If it exists, cite the study itself. If you can't find it, it doesn't go in your paper.

The pattern: almost nothing is simply "good" or "bad." The move is to investigate who made it and why, trace claims to where they started, and ask whose interest it serves. A trade-group page, a news article, even an AI answer can all be useful, as long as you know what each one actually is.

Peer-reviewed

Before a study runs in a scholarly journal, other experts in the field read it and check the methods and the reasoning. That vetting is peer review.

It doesn't make a study perfect, but it means the work cleared a bar a random blog post never faces. That's why peer-reviewed sources are your strongest evidence.

Trade group

An association funded by the companies in an industry to promote their shared interests. The American Egg Board speaks for egg producers.

That doesn't make its pages worthless, but its job is to sell you on its members. Treat its claims as an interested party's, never as a neutral finding.

Primary vs. secondary source

A primary source is the original: the actual study, the data, the document itself. A secondary source is someone reporting on it, like a news article about a study.

Chase the claim back to the primary source and cite that. The retelling can drop details or get it wrong.
