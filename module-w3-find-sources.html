Find & Vet Your Sources

Your first module on your own. Same rhythm as always: read a little, do a little, and it lands in your notebook. Budget about 45 minutes.

Step 1, where to actually look

Regular Google is where research goes to get shallow. Start in places built for real sources:

Belk Library . The good stuff you can't get free on the open web, and it's already paid for by your tuition. library.appstate.edu

Google Scholar. Searches scholarly work specifically. scholar.google.com, and see that "Cited by" link under each result? That's a shortcut to newer work built on it.

AI research tools. Perplexity is strong for research because it searches the live web and shows you its sources. Elicit and Consensus are built specifically to surface real academic papers. General chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude) are best for brainstorming search terms and explaining a hard concept, not for citations.

⚠ The one rule about AI and sources

AI will hand you a confident, perfectly formatted citation for a study that does not exist. It happens constantly. So: use AI to point you toward real work, then go find that work, open it, and read it yourself. Never cite anything you haven't actually read. If you can't find it, it doesn't go in your paper.

A few prompts worth trying (then verify everything they give you):

Find studies on how shoppers interpret "natural" claims on food labels. List each with a link, and only include ones you can confirm exist.
I'm researching [your question]. Suggest 8 search terms and 3 databases I should try.
Put this abstract in plain language for me: [paste it]. (For understanding, not for citing.)

Step 2, the breadcrumb trick

Found one genuinely good source? Open its bibliography. Everything that author cited is a curated list of the best related work, handed to you for free. Follow those breadcrumbs and you'll find five more sources fast.

This is also the deeper reason we cite at all. A reference list isn't busywork or a way to avoid plagiarism, it's a trail of breadcrumbs you leave for the next researcher, so they can retrace your steps, check your claims, and build on or replicate what you did. You're following other people's trails right now. Leave a clear one of your own.

Step 3, how to vet what you find: SIFT

Before you trust any source, run it through four quick moves. This is Mike Caulfield's SIFT method (free to read, and the short version is right here):

  • S
    Stop. Before you read or share, notice what you're looking at and whether you even know the source.
  • I
    Investigate the source. Who made this, and why? A quick search about them (this is , leaving the page to see what others say) tells you more than the page's own "About Us."
  • F
    Find better coverage. Is there a stronger, more independent source for the same claim? Use it.
  • T
    Trace claims, quotes, and data back to where they started. News about a study is not the study. An AI's summary is not the source.
✍ On your own

Our lens question rides along with SIFT: when you investigate a source, ask whose interest it serves and whose voice is missing from the conversation. A trade group and an independent researcher can make the same claim for very different reasons.

Peer-reviewed

Before a study runs in a scholarly journal, other experts in the field read it and check the methods and the reasoning. That vetting is peer review.

It doesn't make a study perfect, but it means the work cleared a bar a random blog post never faces. That's why peer-reviewed sources are your strongest evidence.

Lateral reading

Instead of judging a source by its own slick page, you open new tabs and see what other people say about it. You read across sources, not down the one in front of you.

It's the core SIFT move, and it's how fact-checkers size up an unfamiliar site in seconds.

Database

A library database is a searchable collection of vetted scholarly sources that the library pays for, so it isn't free on the open web. Your tuition already covers it.

Unlike plain Google, what's inside has usually cleared an editorial or peer-review bar, so you start from better material.
