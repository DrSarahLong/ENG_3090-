Find & Vet Your Sources

Your first module on your own over Zoom. Same rhythm as always: read a little, do a little, and it lands in your notebook.

Arrive. Open your Research Notebook in another tab and take a breath. Last week you locked your topic: the texts you'll analyze, the people you could ask, the justice angle. Today you go find the sources that tell you what's already known, and you learn to tell the trustworthy ones from the junk before any of it lands in your paper.

A locked topic is a good question with nowhere to stand yet. Sources are the ground under it. Finding them is easy; deciding which ones to trust is the real skill, and that's today's work.

1 · Where to actually look

Regular Google is where research goes to get shallow. It's built to sell and to please, not to vet. Start instead in places built for real sources, where something has already cleared a bar before it reached you.

  • Belk Library . The good material you can't get free on the open web, already paid for by your tuition. library.appstate.edu
  • Google Scholar. Searches scholarly work specifically. scholar.google.com — and that "Cited by" link under each result is a shortcut to newer work built on it.
  • AI research tools. Perplexity is strong here because it searches the live web and shows you its sources. Elicit and Consensus are built specifically to surface real academic papers. General chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude) are best for brainstorming search terms and explaining a hard concept, not for citations.

⚠ The one rule about AI and sources.

AI will hand you a confident, perfectly formatted citation for a study that does not exist. It happens constantly. So use AI to point you toward real work, then go find that work, open it, and read it yourself. Never cite anything you haven't actually read. If you can't find it, it doesn't go in your paper.

2 · The breadcrumb trick

Found one genuinely good source? Open its bibliography. Everything that author cited is a curated list of the best related work, handed to you for free. Follow those breadcrumbs and you'll find five more sources fast, and better ones than a cold search turns up.

See it

Say a strong article on our food-label example cites a 2019 study on how "organic" is regulated and a 2021 survey of shopper trust. You didn't have to find either one; the author already vetted them and put them in the list. Two clicks, two new leads. Now do that on your best source.

This is also the deeper reason we cite at all. A reference list isn't busywork or a way to dodge plagiarism. It's a trail of breadcrumbs you leave for the next researcher, so they can retrace your steps, check your claims, and build on what you did. You're following other people's trails right now. Leave a clear one of your own.

Your turn

Pick the strongest source you've found so far. Open its bibliography, and in Entry 3 add at least two new leads you got from it. Note which source they came from, so you remember the trail.

3 · Vet what you find: the SIFT moves

A source in your pile is a lead, not yet evidence. Before you trust any of it, run it through four quick moves. This is the SIFT method, and the short version is right here, followed by the real thing.

Read the real thing · 5 min

SIFT (The Four Moves) — Mike Caulfield, a digital-literacy researcher who introduced SIFT in a 2019 post on his blog Hapgood, after years of watching students judge sources by how polished they looked. His four moves are the quick, canonical way to decide whether a source is trustworthy.

  • S
    Stop. Before you read or share, notice what you're looking at and whether you even know the source.
  • I
    Investigate the source. Who made this, and why? A quick search about them (this is , leaving the page to see what others say) tells you more than the page's own "About Us."
  • F
    Find better coverage. Is there a stronger, more independent source for the same claim? Use it.
  • T
    Trace claims, quotes, and data back to where they started. News about a study is not the study. An AI's summary is not the source.

Ride our lens along with SIFT. When you investigate a source, add two questions: whose interest does it serve, and whose voice is missing from the conversation? A trade group and an independent researcher can make the exact same claim for very different reasons. That doesn't make either one useless; it tells you how to weigh it.

Your turn

Back to your sources. Run SIFT on the leads in Entry 3. For each keeper, jot one line on why you trust it and one line on whose interest it serves. Cut anything you can't trace to where the claim started.

Peer-reviewed

Before a study runs in a scholarly journal, other experts in the field read it and check the methods and the reasoning. That vetting is peer review.

It doesn't make a study perfect, but it means the work cleared a bar a random blog post never faces. That's why peer-reviewed sources are your strongest evidence.

Lateral reading

Instead of judging a source by its own slick page, you open new tabs and see what other people say about it. You read across sources, not down the one in front of you.

It's the core SIFT move, and it's how fact-checkers size up an unfamiliar site in seconds. Example: a health site looks official, but one search shows it's run by a supplement company, so now you know how to weigh it.

Database

A library database is a searchable collection of vetted scholarly sources that the library pays for, so it isn't free on the open web. Your tuition already covers it.

Unlike plain Google, what's inside has usually cleared an editorial or peer-review bar, so you start from better material. Example: searching Academic Search Complete through Belk Library instead of Googling the same terms.
