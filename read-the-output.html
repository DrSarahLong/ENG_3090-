Read the Tool's Output

You coded your corpus by hand. A tool like Voyant or AntConc does the counting in seconds, and finds patterns your eye slides past. Here's what it hands back, and how to read it. (Short texts like labels won't give a tool much, which is why you want longer texts, like news articles.)

1. Word frequency

Feed your searchable text to the tool and it counts every word. Here's the list from a set of a dozen news articles about food labeling (skipping "the," "a," and other filler):

Top words · 12 news articles

natural61
label52
food46
organic38
consumers33
companies28
claims22
regulators16
Read it: the words at the top are the story, in numbers. These articles circle "natural," "label," and "organic" over and over. And notice what sits low or missing: "regulators" is way down, and "worker" and "cost" barely register. What a corpus doesn't say is data too.

2. The same list, as a picture

Most tools also draw a word cloud, where bigger means more frequent. Same data, faster to feel:

natural label food organic consumers companies claims regulators health
Careful: a word cloud is pretty and a little dumb. It shows size, not meaning, and it strips every word from its context. Great for a first look, never the whole analysis.

3. Concordance: how a word is actually used

This is the powerful one. A (or "KWIC") pulls every appearance of a word with the words around it, so you see how it's used, not just how often:

Concordance · "natural"

the wordnaturalhas no legal meaning
shoppers assumenaturalmeans healthier or safer
companies putnaturalon almost anything
critics saynaturalis marketing, not science
unlike organic,naturalis barely regulated
Read it: in these articles "natural" keeps company with means, assume, regulated, word, marketing, those are its . Notice the difference from a label: here the corpus isn't using the word to sell, it's interrogating it. The collocates tell you at a glance that this is a debate about the label, not an ad.

So what? (still your job)

The tool counted, drew, and pulled context in seconds. What it cannot do is tell you that "natural" is legally unregulated, or that this mood works on a tired shopper, or whose interest it serves. That reading is yours. Use the tool to find the pattern fast, then do the thinking the tool can't.

Word frequency

A simple count of how often each word appears in your corpus, usually after tossing out filler words like "the" and "of." It's the fastest way to see what a set of texts keeps coming back to. Tools like Voyant and AntConc make it in seconds.

Concordance (KWIC)

A list of every place a word appears, shown with the words on either side, so you can read how it's actually used. KWIC stands for "keyword in context." It's the single most useful thing a text tool does: frequency tells you how often, a concordance tells you how.

Collocation

The words that tend to show up right next to a given word, its regular company. If "natural" almost always travels with "fresh," "family," and "goodness," those collocates tell you what the word is really doing (setting a wholesome mood), even when the word itself claims nothing.
