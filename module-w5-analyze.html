4 · Take your first pass

Now read through your own corpus once, noticing, not judging yet. This first pass is the start of : spotting the moves that repeat. Look for:

  • Recurring words and phrases. What language shows up again and again?
  • . What picture does the wording paint? What feeling does it reach for?
  • Present and absent. What do these texts always mention? What do they never say?
  • The appeals. Emotion, nature, authority, family, tradition, fear.
  • Whose interest. Who benefits if you believe this language? Who's missing from it?

Reading our carton corpus, you might notice the word "fresh" on nearly every label and "cage" almost never, even though cages are the thing shoppers most want to know about. That gap, always "fresh," never "cage," is a pattern. It's rough, unproven, and exactly the kind of hunch a first pass is supposed to surface.

Discourse analysis

A close, systematic look at how real texts make meaning: word choices, framing, what they include and leave out, and how they move power. You read like a detective to see what the language is doing, not just what it says. (Linguist James Paul Gee, whose An Introduction to Discourse Analysis appeared in 1999, treats language as always doing something; Norman Fairclough's "critical discourse analysis," named in the 1990s, focuses on how it does power.)

Reading how a carton says "farm fresh" is exactly this: watching language do work in the world.

Content analysis

The close cousin of discourse analysis that leans on counting: how often a word, theme, or move appears across your texts. Discourse analysis asks "how is this language working?"; content analysis asks "how often, and where?" Most good projects use a little of both. (Klaus Krippendorff's Content Analysis, first published in 1980, is the standard handbook.)

Example: counting that "fresh" appears on 14 of 15 cartons and "cage" on 2 is content analysis; explaining what that silence around cages does is discourse analysis.

Corpus

Your set of texts to analyze, gathered on purpose. Aim for 15 to 20 items of the same kind (news articles, labels, ads, policies, posts) so real patterns show up instead of one-off quirks. "Corpus" just means a "body" of texts. Corpus linguists like Paul Baker, whose Using Corpora in Discourse Analysis came out in 2006, study language exactly this way.

Example: fifteen egg-carton front labels copied into one Google Doc is a corpus; a single label is not.

Coding

Tagging your texts for the things that repeat, a recurring word, a type of appeal, a framing, so you can see patterns instead of drowning in detail. On your first pass you're just noticing; later you name your patterns and count how often each shows up. (Johnny Saldaña's The Coding Manual for Qualitative Researchers, first published in 2009, is the go-to guide.)

Example: marking every carton that uses a nature word (fresh, farm, pasture, natural) with the tag "nature appeal" is one line of coding.

Framing

How word choice steers what you notice and feel. Call something "farm fresh" and you picture a meadow; call the same eggs "mass-produced" and you don't. From the news: "estate tax" and "death tax" name the same policy but pull opposite feelings. (Sociologist Erving Goffman named framing in his 1974 book Frame Analysis; linguist George Lakoff, from the 2000s on, showed how politics runs on it.)

OCR (optical character recognition)

Software that reads the letters inside an image or a scanned PDF and turns them back into real, searchable text. Your phone's camera, Google Drive, and Adobe Acrobat can all do it. You need OCR whenever your source is a picture of words rather than the words themselves.

Example: a photo of an egg carton is just pixels to a computer until OCR pulls "Grade A Large · Farm Fresh" out of it as text you can search and count.
