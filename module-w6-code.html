You built a codebook on Tuesday. Today you run it across your whole corpus, count what you find, and say what the pattern means.

Arrive. Open your Research Notebook in another tab and take a breath. On Tuesday you built your in Entry 6a: your named codes, each with a definition and an example. Today you put it to work on every text you've gathered. There's no new theory to learn, this is the day the setup pays off.

The codebook was the careful, slow half, and you've done it. Now it goes to work. By the end you'll have counts, a pattern, and one sentence that turns a pile of texts into an argument.

1 · Code your whole corpus

Go through every text in your corpus and tag it with the from your codebook. A single text can carry more than one. Keep your definitions right next to you so you apply each code the same way on every text, that consistency is what makes your results rather than just your mood on a given afternoon.

A simple table does the whole job. One row per text, one column per code, a check where it applies. A plain-text file, a Google Sheet, or a table inside your notebook Doc all work fine.

See it — our running example

For the egg-carton corpus, one carton's front panel might get tagged both appeal to nature ("farm fresh") and vague virtue ("wholesome goodness"). The next carton gets only appeal to nature. Same two definitions, applied the same way down the whole stack. That's coding.

The carton is our example all semester, not your topic. Watch the move, then run it on the texts you actually gathered.

2 · Count what you found

Now tally it. How many of your texts use each code? This is the side of the work, the numbers that turn "it felt like a lot" into "12 of 15." The count isn't the analysis yet, but it's the evidence the analysis will stand on, so get it exact.

See it — our running example

Across fifteen cartons: appeal to nature shows up on 12, vague virtue on 9, and a real, checkable claim (a certification with a standard behind it) on only 2. Written plainly: "12 of 15 cartons lean on nature words; 2 of 15 make a claim you could actually verify."

Your turn

In Entry 6b, write your tally: each code and how many of your texts carry it. Small corpus? Even "5 of 6" is a finding.

3 · Interpret — say what it means

Here's where the grade lives. Counting is the easy part; is the point. Your counts are facts. Interpretation says what those facts mean. Work these three questions against your numbers:

  • 1
    What does the dominant pattern mean? Not "appeal to nature appears 12 times," but what that tells us about how these texts work on a reader.
  • 2
    Whose interest does it serve? Who benefits if the pattern works on people, and who pays?
  • 3
    What's absent? What do these texts never say? Silence is data too.
See it — the three questions, worked

What it means: the corpus mostly sells a feeling of "nature" instead of any checkable fact. Whose interest: that serves brands, who get a premium for a warm word, at the expense of shoppers with the least time to read fine print. What's absent: almost no carton names an actual standard or who audits it. The silence is the strategy.

The move that makes it an analysis. Finish this sentence, backed by your counts: "Across my corpus, the language mostly does ___, which serves ___ at the expense of ___." That one sentence is the spine of your P2. Everything else in the write-up supports it.

Your turn

In Entry 6b, write your own "so what" sentence for your corpus, then read it out loud. If it only restates the count, it isn't done. Push it until it says something a reader couldn't have guessed without your analysis.

A code

One named category in your analysis, like "appeal to nature," with a short definition and a clear example so you apply it the same way every time. Codes are the buckets you sort your texts into.

Example: the code appeal to nature = "words that sell a feeling of natural/wholesome/pure with nothing checkable behind them," and it catches phrases like "farm fresh" or "the way it was meant to be." (Johnny Saldaña's Coding Manual for Qualitative Researchers, 2009, is the standard guide.)

Codebook

Your full list of codes, each with its definition and an example. It's what makes your analysis systematic rather than just your impression, and it lets someone else code the same texts and land close to your results.

Example: a three-code codebook might hold appeal to nature, vague virtue, and verifiable claim, each with a one-line definition and a sample phrase, so every text gets judged by the same yardstick.

Intercoder reliability

The check that your coding isn't just your opinion: if another person used your codebook on the same texts, would they tag them the same way? The tighter your definitions, the more your results hold up.

Example: if two people both tag "farm fresh" as appeal to nature, that's high agreement. If one calls it appeal to nature and the other verifiable claim, your definition needs sharpening. You don't need a formal test for P2, but coding consistently is the same idea.

Content analysis

The counting cousin of discourse analysis: how often each code appears across your corpus. Discourse analysis asks "how is this working?"; content analysis asks "how often, and where?" Strong projects use both.

Example: noticing that a carton sells a feeling instead of a fact is discourse analysis; reporting that 12 of 15 cartons do it is content analysis. (Klaus Krippendorff's Content Analysis, first edition 1980, is the standard handbook.)

Interpretation

The step that turns findings into meaning. Your counts are facts; interpretation says what they mean, why the pattern is there, whose interest it serves, and what it does to a reader.

Example: "appeal to nature: 12 of 15" is a finding. "These cartons sell a feeling of nature because a warm word earns a premium a real standard can't" is interpretation. It's the difference between a report and an analysis, and it's where the real thinking (and the grade) lives.
