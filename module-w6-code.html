Apply Your Codebook & Interpret

You built a codebook on Tuesday (Entry 6a). Now you run it across your whole corpus and, most important, say what the pattern means. Budget about 45 minutes.

Step 1: code the whole corpus

Go through every text in your corpus and tag it with the from your . A text can carry more than one. Keep your definitions next to you so you apply each code the same way every time, that consistency is what makes your results .

Tip: a simple table does the job. One row per text, one column per code, a check where it applies. A plain-text file, a Google Sheet, or a table in your Doc all work.

Step 2: count

Now tally it up: how many of your texts use each code? This is the side, the numbers that turn "it felt like a lot" into "12 of 15." Note which codes are common, which are rare, and any that surprised you.

Step 3: interpret (this is the analysis)

Here's where the grade lives. Counting is the easy part; is the point. Ask:

  • What does the dominant pattern mean? Not "appeal to nature appears 12 times," but what that tells us about how these texts work on a reader.
  • Whose interest does it serve? Who benefits if the pattern works, and who pays?
  • What's absent? What do these texts never say? Silence is data too.
The move that makes it an analysis

Finish this, backed by your counts: "Across my corpus, the language mostly does ___, which serves ___ at the expense of ___." That sentence is the spine of your P2.

Step 4: pull your evidence

Grab 2 or 3 exact quotes from your texts that show the pattern in action. In your write-up, every claim you make will lean on a quote like these. No paraphrasing the evidence; use the real words.

✍ On your own

Read your "so what" sentence out loud. If it only restates the count, it isn't done yet. Push it until it says something a reader couldn't have guessed without your analysis.

A code

One named category in your analysis, like "appeal to nature," with a short definition and a clear example so you apply it the same way every time. Codes are the buckets you sort your texts into. (Johnny Saldaña's Coding Manual for Qualitative Researchers is the standard guide.)

Codebook

Your full list of codes, each with its definition and an example. It's what makes your analysis systematic rather than just your impression, and it lets someone else code the same texts and land close to your results.

Intercoder reliability

The check that your coding isn't just your opinion: if another person used your codebook on the same texts, would they tag them the same way? The tighter your definitions, the more your results hold up. You don't need a formal test for P2, but coding consistently is the same idea.

Content analysis

The counting cousin of discourse analysis: how often each code appears across your corpus. Discourse analysis asks "how is this working?"; content analysis asks "how often, and where?" Strong projects use both. (Klaus Krippendorff's Content Analysis is the standard handbook.)

Interpretation

The step that turns findings into meaning. Your counts are facts; interpretation says what they mean, why the pattern is there, whose interest it serves, and what it does to a reader. It's the difference between a report and an analysis, and it's where the real thinking (and the grade) lives.
