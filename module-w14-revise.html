Revise, Review & Polish

You have a full draft. This week you turn it into the final. That takes real revision, seeing the whole thing again, not just fixing typos. Budget about ninety minutes across your draft.

First, permission. Your draft is allowed to be rough right now. The writer Anne Lamott has a famous name for the first version of anything: the . Her point is that nobody, not published researchers, not me, not you, writes a good draft first. You write a messy one so you have something to make good. The report you hand in next week won't be the one you have now, and that's exactly how it's supposed to work.

Step 1: revise, edit, and proofread are three different jobs

Most students do only the last two and call it revising. Do all three, and do them big to small:

(ideas & structure). Is the argument clear? Is anything missing, repeated, or out of order? Does each section do its job? This is where a draft actually gets better.
Edit (sentences). Tighten wordy sentences, fix awkward phrasing, smooth transitions, warm up the academic tone.
Proofread (errors). Typos, citation formatting, page numbers, figure captions. Last, because there's no sense polishing a paragraph you might cut.

Step 2: fix the big things first

Writing centers call this . Sort your problems and start at the top:

Higher-order (do first): focus and argument, organization, whether each claim has evidence, whether the report answers its own question.

Lower-order (do after): grammar, word choice, citation style, formatting, typos.

There's no point perfecting the commas in a paragraph you're about to delete. Structure first, sentences second, specks last.

Step 3: X-ray your structure with a reverse outline

The fastest way to see your report's real bones is to it: go paragraph by paragraph and write, in one line, the job that paragraph does. Line them up and the gaps, repetition, and out-of-order pieces jump out. You did this in class; do it again on your latest draft.

Step 4: read your own draft like a skeptical stranger

You know what you meant to say, which is exactly why you can't see what's missing. Read your draft as a tired grader who knows nothing about your project would, against the same things it'll be judged on:

Question & significanceIs there a clear research question, and is it obvious why it matters?
Sections & structureAll of IMRAD present, in order, each doing its job? Results reports; Discussion interprets?
EvidenceDoes every claim have support (a number, a source, a quote)? Are figures captioned?
HonestyAre limitations named? Do claims match the evidence (suggests, not proves)?
Coherence & voiceDo the sections connect? Is the tone consistent and academic throughout?
Design & mechanicsHeadings, page numbers, consistent citations (15 to 20 sources), clean prose?

Step 5: read it aloud

Your eyes skip what your ears catch. is one of the best ways to find clunky sentences, missing words, and places where two ideas don't connect. Every spot you stumble is a spot your reader will too. Mark them and fix them.

The research: in a 2025 study, students who read their drafts aloud corrected about three errors per ten-minute paragraph that they'd otherwise have missed (Eckstein, Lung & Gillette, TESL-EJ, open access). A free, five-minute habit with a real payoff.

✍ On your own

Find the weakest paragraph in your draft, the one you'd least want a stranger to read first. Don't fix a comma in it. Ask what job it's supposed to do, and whether it does it. Then revise it to do that job, or cut it.

Shitty first drafts

From Anne Lamott's Bird by Bird (1994). She (via a friend) calls the first draft the "down draft," where you just get it down; the second the "up draft," where you fix it up; and the third the "dental draft," where you check every tooth. The whole idea: let yourself write badly first, because you can't revise a blank page. Read the essay (free PDF).

Revision vs. editing

Different jobs. Revision ("re-seeing") works on ideas and structure: what's missing, repeated, out of order, unclear. Editing works on sentences; proofreading catches errors. Do them big to small. Most weak final drafts were edited and proofread but never truly revised, the sentences are clean but the structure was never re-seen.

Higher- vs. lower-order concerns

A writing-center framework. Higher-order concerns: focus, argument, organization, development, audience, the stuff that decides whether the report works. Lower-order concerns: grammar, spelling, citations, formatting. Fix higher-order first. Polishing sentences in a section you'll end up cutting is wasted effort.

Reverse outlining

Outlining a draft you've already written to reveal its real structure. Go paragraph by paragraph and note, in one line, the job each does. Laid out together, the outline exposes gaps, repetition, and bad order that finished prose hides. It's the most reliable revision tool there is, because rearranging a skeleton beats rewriting polished paragraphs.

Reading aloud

Reading your draft out loud (or having your device read it to you) forces you to process every word instead of skimming what you expect to see. It's uncannily good at surfacing clunky sentences, missing words, and joints where two ideas don't actually connect. If you stumble reading it, your reader will stumble too. A 2025 study found reading aloud caught about three errors per ten-minute paragraph (Eckstein, Lung & Gillette, TESL-EJ).
