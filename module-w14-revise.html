Why we are doing this

This is rehearsal for the room where someone decides whether to hire you, fund your cause, hand you a campaign, or admit you to graduate school. The people making those decisions are mostly Gen X and Boomers, and they learned a different set of presentation rules than you did.

Here is one gap in perception as an example:

Their side. In a December 2023 survey of 800 U.S. hiring managers, more than half said Gen Z candidates struggle most with eye contact in interviews, and 21% said some candidates refused to turn on their cameras in virtual interviews (Intelligent survey, reported by Fox).

Your side. In a 2023 survey of 1,015 Gen Z workers, 44% said they are uneasy or nervous about delivering presentations at work, and 42% named soft skills training as the top thing they want from employers (Kahoot! and Researchscape survey, reported by Agility PR).

By the end of today you will have: one takeaway sentence, a six-beat map of your talk, two planned audience questions, and a memorized first and last line.

Part 1 · Start with one sentence

A talk is not your project read aloud. It is one idea, delivered so the room remembers it. Think of a TED or TEDx talk. The whole format is built on a single “idea worth spreading,” and the talks people remember can be summed up in a sentence. Yours should work the same way. Fill this in before you open Canva.

The basics

Who is listening?

Your classmates are in the room, but that’s not really your audience. Write for the person who will one day decide whether to hire, fund, or promote you. Picture someone your professor’s age in the back row.

Part 2 · Map your six beats

Every strong talk moves through the same six beats. Write keywords only, never full sentences. If you write sentences, you will read them, and the idea is to give a talk, not a reading.

1. Hook0:45

Earn attention with a number, a question, or a short moment

2. Question and why it matters1:30

The gap in the literature, who it affects, and your justice lens

3. What I did2:00

Both methods: your corpus and codebook, then your instrument, sample, and recruitment

4. What I found4:00

One qualitative and one quantitative finding, each with a visual or a quotation

5. So what2:00

Specific recommendations tied to your findings, plus one limitation you raise yourself

6. Close0:30

Takeaway again, one memorable last line, thank you

Your window is 10 to 12 minutes, talks run long, and going over reads as unprepared, which is why following this script should land you somewhere around 10 minutes 45 seconds.

The beats are a compressed IMRAD. Beat 3 is your Methods, beat 4 your Results, beat 5 your Discussion.

Your rubric: the report is graded on integration, so the talk should sound like one study, not three projects in a row. Say how the literature review led to the discourse analysis, and how that led to the survey.

Script only two sentences

Memorize these. Everything else you speak from keywords.

Part 3 · Turn the audience into participants

The video we watched argues for building questions into your talk. A question changes the audience’s job from sitting through it to thinking with you. In a 10 to 12 minute talk, plan two or three and space them out. Each one should cost you under 30 seconds.

Type of questionExampleBest beat
Show of hands“Raise your hand if you have ever…”Hook
Guess before the reveal“What percent of respondents do you think said yes?”Just before your key finding
This or thatShow two versions. “Which one would you stop scrolling for?”Finding or example
Think, do not answer“Ask yourself when you last…”Hook or So what
Seed the Q&A“The thing I still can’t explain is… I’d like your read on it.”Close

Rules that make questions work

  • Make it answerable in five seconds.
  • Ask, then wait three full seconds. Count them silently. It will feel long to you but normal to them.
  • Do something with the answer.
  • Have a line ready for silence.
  • Online presenters: tell people exactly how to answer. “Type 1 or 2 in the chat.”
If a wrong answer would derail you, turn it into a think-about-it question: “Ask yourself what you would have guessed.”

Part 4 · Build it in Canva

Use Canva. It is free through the university, the templates handle fonts and spacing for you, and it runs in a browser on any classroom computer. Do not open it until Parts 1 to 3 are filled in. Slides come last because they support the talk. They are not the talk.

Setup

Slide rules

  • One idea per slide. If a slide makes two points, it is two slides.
  • Six words is plenty. The audience cannot read and listen at the same time, and they will choose reading.
  • One visual per finding, shown large, with a caption. Example: “Figure 1. Survey responses by age group, N = 42.”
  • Type readable from the back row: 28 point or larger. No screenshots of tiny tables.
  • No animations, sound effects, or memes unless one is evidence. A decision-maker reads decoration as padding.
  • Final slide: your takeaway sentence, not “Questions?” or “Thank you.” It stays on screen through the whole Q&A.

Part 5 · Say it so they listen

Writing for the ear is different from writing for the page. A reader can go back. A listener cannot.

Write for the ear

  • Short sentences. One breath each.
  • Signposts. “Two findings. First…” tells the room where they are.
  • Make numbers human. “62%” becomes “nearly two out of three people in this room.”
  • One specific person, post, or moment beats three general statements.
  • Repeat the takeaway. On the page that is redundant. Out loud it is how people remember.

Part 6 · End on purpose

The last thing you say is the thing the room carries out the door. Most talks waste it on “So, yeah, that’s it” or “Any questions?” A planned ending tells a decision-maker you were in control from the first word to the last.

Type of endingHow it worksExample
Circle backReturn to the person, number, or moment from your hook and show what has changed“I opened with one family’s water bill. The next one is due in three weeks.”
The takeaway, said plainlyYour one sentence from Part 1, slower and with nothing after it“This debate comes down to one question: who gets asked.”
The askTell the room exactly what should happen next“Before the next vote, put this survey in front of the people who live closest.”
Look aheadLeave them with a picture of what happens if your recommendation is followed, or ignored“In five years someone will study what this county decided. I want them to find that residents were heard.”

Rules for the last line

  • Make it a statement, not a question.
  • Keep it under 15 words so you can say it without looking down.
  • Say it, pause for two full seconds, then say “Thank you.” Questions come after that.
Never end on your limitations, your sources, or an apology.
