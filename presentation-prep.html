Plan Your Talk

Your presentation isn't your report read aloud. It's about five minutes to make one point land. Build the talk here, beat by beat, and it writes your speaker notes for you. Everything stays on your device.

A research talk is a compressed : hook, question, what you did, what you found, so what, close. Don't try to say everything, you can't. Pick the you want the room to remember, and build every beat to serve it.

Build your six beats

A number, a question, or a quick real moment. Not "Hi, my project is about…"

Your talk, on one screen

Ready to present?

  • I have one clear takeaway, and I say it out loud in the talk.
  • My best figure is on a slide, big and .
  • I can say it without from a script.
  • I have a strong first line and a strong last line.
  • I and timed it, it's under five minutes.
  • I can answer "what would you do differently?" and "what surprised you?"

The nerves are normal. You're the person in the room who knows the most about your study, nobody else collected your data or read your labels. You're not performing; you're telling us what you found. Know your first line cold, breathe, and talk to us like people.

A talk is a compressed IMRAD

Your report's structure still runs underneath your talk, just shorter. Hook and question do the work of the Introduction; "what you did" is the Methods; "what you found" is the Results; "so what" is the Discussion. Same arc, five minutes, one main point.

One takeaway

People forget almost everything in a five-minute talk except one idea, if you're lucky. So choose that idea on purpose and repeat it, near the start and again at the end. If your audience remembers only your takeaway, the talk worked. Trying to convey everything usually conveys nothing.

Show one strong visual

Put your single best chart or table on a slide, large, with its caption ("Figure 1. …, N = 42"). A clear visual does more in three seconds than a paragraph of talking. Skip slides full of text; the audience can't read and listen at once. One figure they can grasp beats five bullet points they can't.

Talk, don't read

Reading a script word for word flattens your voice and loses the room. Use a few notes (like the ones this tool makes) as reminders, not a script, and speak to your audience. You know this material better than anyone here; trust that. Looking up and talking to people beats a perfect paragraph read to the floor.

Practice out loud and time it

Rehearse the whole thing aloud at least twice, on a clock. It's the only way to know your real length (talks always run long) and to smooth the rough spots before you're in front of people. Practicing in your head doesn't count; your mouth needs the reps. Aim to finish comfortably under your limit.
