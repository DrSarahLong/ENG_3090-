1 · Why these rules exist

Every rule in research ethics is written in someone's harm. The Nuremberg Code (1947) followed the Nazi medical experiments. The Tuskegee syphilis study (1932 to 1972) let hundreds of Black men go untreated for decades without their informed consent. Out of that came the (1979), which set the three principles all human-subjects research still follows.

2 · The three principles, applied to you

Those three words from the reading are the whole framework. Here's what each one asks of a study like yours.

  • Respect for persons. People decide freely whether to join, with full information. That's .
  • Beneficence. Do good, and minimize harm. Ask yourself what could go wrong for a participant, and prevent it.
  • Justice. Share the burdens and benefits fairly. Don't run your study on the people who can least afford to say no. (Sound familiar? It's our justice lens, pointed at your own methods.)
See it — in our running example

Say you're surveying shoppers about the sustainability claims on egg cartons. Respect means telling them it's a class research survey and letting them decline. Beneficence means not secretly recording them or asking anything that could embarrass them. Justice means not cornering, say, the low-wage stockers on the clock, who can least afford to brush you off, and calling that a fair sample.

The egg carton is our running example all semester, not your topic. Watch the move, then run it on your own study.

An egg-carton survey needs to know whether someone reads the claims and how they read them. It does not need their name, email, or address. Drop those fields and your survey is anonymous by design: there's nothing to leak, because you never collected it.

6 · Write what you'll actually say

Two short pieces of writing make your study real: a recruitment pitch (the invitation) and a consent statement (what people read before they agree). You don't have to invent the consent form from scratch. Model it on App State's consent templates (use the exempt research one, since that's us).

The Belmont Report

A 1979 U.S. report, written after abuses like the Tuskegee study, that set the three principles all human-subjects research now follows: respect for persons (informed, voluntary participation), beneficence (maximize good, minimize harm), and justice (fairly share risks and benefits, don't exploit the vulnerable). Your CITI training is built on it.

Informed consent

Before anyone takes part, they get a plain-language explanation of what the study is, what you'll ask, how their data will be used and protected, and that they can stop anytime, and then they freely agree. "Informed" and "voluntary" are the whole idea. No consent, no data.

Example: a one-paragraph note at the top of your survey that says who you are, what the survey is about, how long it takes, that it's anonymous, and that they can quit anytime.

Anonymous vs. confidential

Not the same thing. Anonymous means you never collect anything that could identify a person, not even you can link a response to a name. Confidential means you might know who said what, but you protect it and never reveal it. If your form asks for a name or email, you can only promise confidentiality.

Example: a survey with no name field is anonymous; an interview where you know who you talked to is, at best, confidential.

IRB (Institutional Review Board)

A committee at a university that reviews research with human participants before it begins, to keep it ethical. The key thing for you: student projects for a class at this level are exempt, so you will not go through IRB review. That's also why some topics are off the table: you can't study people in jails, schoolchildren, or other protected groups, because those need a full review a class project can't do. A short anonymous survey of adults is exactly the kind of thing that's fine.

CITI

The Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative, the standard online training in research ethics that most universities require before you work with human participants. You read short modules and pass quizzes (80% or higher), then download a completion certificate that's good for about three years.

Example: the Social & Behavioral Research course you'll complete this week is the same training real researchers do, so it's another line for your resume.
