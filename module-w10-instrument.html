Designing Your Instrument

In class you learned to spot a broken question. Now you build the whole thing: the survey or interview that will actually get you data worth analyzing. This is the start of P3. Budget about an hour.

Notice what just happened. Up to now you've been a : reading, evaluating, and analyzing work other people already made, your literature review, your analysis of food labels. Starting this week you become a . You generate data that doesn't exist yet, and this instrument is the hinge the whole study turns on. That's a real promotion, and it raises the stakes on getting these questions right.

Step 1: what an instrument is, and why it's the whole ballgame

Your is the tool you use to collect data: a survey, an interview guide, a set of focus-group questions. It's the most important thing you'll build in P3, because everything downstream depends on it. You can't fix a bad question with clever analysis. If the question was leading or vague, the numbers it produced are leading and vague too. Garbage in, garbage out. So we spend real time here. And notice: the best instrument isn't invented from nothing. It's built to answer the exact gap you found in your literature review back in P1. The parts of this project are meant to talk to each other.

Step 2: pick your method

  • . The workhorse, and what most of you will use. You reach a lot of people fast, and the answers come back countable, which you'll need for the visualizing step. Best when you want breadth.
  • . Fewer people, much deeper. You get the "why" behind the numbers, in their own words. Best when you want richness, and you're willing to do the slower work of analyzing what they say.
  • . A guided group conversation. Powerful, but harder to schedule, consent, and record cleanly. For a class project it's usually more than you need, choose it only if the group dynamic is genuinely your point.

For P3, pick one of these and do it well. One clean survey (or one solid set of interviews) is exactly the right scope for this project, and it's what almost all of you should do. You can combine methods, a survey plus two or three short interviews, and it's a genuinely strong move. Be honest with yourself first, though: it's ambitious. Doable, and real research, but it's twice the recruiting, twice the consent, twice the analysis. Only reach for it if your one method is already rock-solid and you truly have the time. When in doubt, one method, done right.

Step 3: closed questions to count, open questions to quote

Most of your survey should be questions, multiple choice or a , because in Week 12 you have to turn answers into charts, and you can only chart what you can count. Then add one or two questions to catch the good quotes and the things you didn't think to ask about. A useful shape: mostly closed, a couple open, never all open.

Step 4: build a scale that doesn't lie

When you use a , three rules keep it honest:

  • Balance it. As much room to be negative as positive. No "Excellent / Very good / Good / Fair" one-sided ladders.
  • Give it a middle. An odd number of points (usually 5) so people who are genuinely neutral have somewhere to go.
  • Label every point, not just the ends. "Sometimes" and "Often" mean more to a respondent than a bare 3 and 4.

Step 6: order matters

The sequence of questions shapes who finishes and how honestly. A reliable order:

  1. Consent and intro. What the study is, that it's voluntary and anonymous, how long it takes. (You wrote this in Week 9.)
  2. An easy warm-up. A simple, non-threatening question to get them rolling.
  3. Your core questions. The real ones, grouped by topic so it flows.
  4. Sensitive and questions last. Age, income, and the like go at the end, when trust is highest and a drop-off costs you the least. Only ask what your analysis will actually use.
  5. A thank-you. Close the loop. It's courtesy, and it's good research manners.

Step 8: pilot it before you send it

Every real researcher . Give your draft to one person, a roommate, a friend, and watch. Time it. Note every place they hesitate, re-read a question, or ask "what do you mean?" Those are your broken questions showing themselves while it's still cheap to fix them. Fix, then send it for real next week.

✍ On your own

Read your draft instrument out loud as if you were the participant, a stranger who's doing you a favor and could quit at any moment. Where would you roll your eyes, get confused, or feel pushed toward an answer? Fix those spots first.

Primary vs. secondary research

Secondary research means working with what already exists: reading, evaluating, and analyzing sources other people produced. Your literature review and your analysis of food labels were both secondary. Primary research means generating new data yourself, by surveying, interviewing, or observing real people. When you design an instrument and collect responses, you stop being only a reader of research and become an investigator who produces it. Your final report leans on both: secondary work to find the gap, primary work to fill it.

Instrument

The research tool you use to collect data from people: a survey, an interview guide, or a set of focus-group questions. In your final IMRAD report, a copy of your instrument goes in the appendix so readers can see exactly what you asked. It's the difference between "I asked around" and "here is precisely how I measured this."

Survey

A set of standardized questions given to many people the same way. Its strength is breadth and countable answers: you can chart what a hundred people said. Its limit is depth, you learn what people picked, not always why. The best surveys are short, mostly closed-ended, and ruthlessly clear.

Interview

A guided one-on-one conversation built from open questions. You reach fewer people but go far deeper, and you get answers in their own words, which is where the surprising, quotable material lives. Plan a short interview guide (five to eight questions) and let follow-ups happen naturally.

Focus group

A small group (roughly five to eight people) discussing your topic with you facilitating. Useful when the interaction itself is the data, how people build on or push back against each other. It's harder to schedule, consent, and record than a survey or interview, so for a class project it's the advanced option, not the default.

Mixed methods

Combining approaches, most often some numbers (a survey) and some words (interviews or open responses), so each covers the other's blind spot. Two things to keep straight. Across your whole project you're already doing mixed methods: your P2 was qualitative and your P3 is quantitative, and the final report weaves them together. Within P3 itself, pick one data-collection method and do it well. Running a full survey and full interviews at once is ambitious for one project, take it on only if your main method is already rock-solid.

Closed vs. open questions

Closed questions give set answer choices (multiple choice, a scale), so responses are countable and easy to chart. Open questions let people answer in their own words, richer, but slow to analyze. For P3 you want mostly closed questions (you have to count and visualize) plus a few open ones for quotes and surprises.

Likert scale

The classic agree-to-disagree (or never-to-always) scale, named for psychologist Rensis Likert. Use an odd number of points so there's a genuine neutral middle, label every point (not just the ends), and keep the two sides balanced. It turns an opinion into a number you can average, chart, and compare, exactly what you'll do in Week 12.

Leading question

Worded so it nudges the respondent toward a particular answer ("Don't you agree that…"). It inflates whatever you hoped to find. Fix with neutral wording and a balanced set of choices, so agreeing and disagreeing feel equally allowed.

Double-barreled question

One question that asks about two things at once ("the accuracy and the price"). A single answer can't cover both cleanly. If an "and" or "or" joins two ideas you'd measure separately, split it into two questions.

Loaded question

Assumes something the respondent hasn't agreed to ("When did you stop…?" assumes they stopped). Anyone the assumption doesn't fit is stuck. Ask the prior question first, then branch to the follow-up only for the people it applies to.

Vague question

Built on undefined words ("sustainable," "often," "healthy") that people read differently, so the same answer means different things. Define your terms in the question, or swap a fuzzy word for a concrete, countable one.

Jargon

Technical or academic language that belongs in your write-up, not in front of participants. If people have to decode the question, some guess and some quit. Write survey questions at the reading level of the person answering.

Balanced response scale

The answer choices should offer as much room to be negative as positive. A scale that runs "Excellent · Very good · Good · Fair" has no bottom end, so it collects praise by design. Balance it (very negative → neutral → very positive) so the real distribution can show up.

Demographic questions

Questions about who the respondent is, age, gender, income, region, and so on. They let you compare groups ("do older shoppers trust labels more?"), but they can feel personal, so they go at the end and stay optional. Only ask for a demographic if your analysis will actually use it; otherwise you're collecting risk for nothing.

Pilot testing

Running your instrument past one or two people before the real thing, to catch confusing wording, broken logic, and how long it actually takes. Professionals never skip it, because it's far cheaper to fix a question now than to discover after fifty responses that everyone misread it. Watch where your tester hesitates; that's a bug.
