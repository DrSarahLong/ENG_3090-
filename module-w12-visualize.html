Making the Data Visible

Your responses become findings. This is the module that decides whether your Results section is fair to the people who answered you.

Arrive. Open your Research Notebook and your exported responses in other tabs, and have your actual survey or focus-group questions in front of you. As the , you are the first person to see what your data says. Today is about saying it accurately, showing it clearly, and putting your numbers and your participants' words in the same room. P3 is due Sunday by 11:59 p.m.

On Tuesday you watched two people turn numbers into things a human being can perceive, and you wrote down four ethical rules on the From Data to Display worksheet. Today you apply those rules to your own data, and you build the architecture of your Results section before all of your results are even in.

1 · Ethics did not end when you finished collecting data

You did your CITI training. You got consent. You tried to write neutral questions. Good. You are still completely capable of treating your participants unethically in the Results section.

Suppose 40 people take your homelessness survey, and 22 of them identify housing costs as an important cause of homelessness. You write:

What you wroteAmericans believe housing costs are the primary cause of homelessness.
What your data supportsOf the 40 respondents in this study, 22 identified housing costs as an important contributor to homelessness.

What is wrong with the first one? Everything after the number. Your participants were not “Americans.” They were 40 people you managed to recruit, and most of them were probably App State students. Your data can only represent the population you actually sampled.

Rule one: do not make your data carry more weight than it can hold.

If your sample is 37 students, write Of the 37 respondents in this study… Not People today believe… Not Society thinks… Not Americans agree…

Small samples and false precision

Imagine you have 17 responses and eleven respondents choose housing affordability. You could write 64.7% of respondents… Is that mathematically correct? Yes. Does 64.7% make a convenience sample of 17 people scientific? No. A decimal place can manufacture a sense of precision the sample cannot support.

Hides the scale64.7% of respondents identified housing affordability.
Shows the scale11 of 17 respondents (65%) identified housing affordability.

With a small sample, report both. Your reader deserves to know how many human beings are hiding behind the percentage.

Missing responses are part of the truth

Suppose 40 people took your survey but only 31 answered Question 8. Your graph should not quietly imply that N = 40. Say so: n = 31, or Nine respondents skipped this question. Denominators are part of the data, not a footnote to it.

And do not turn correlation into causation

If students who report knowing someone who has experienced homelessness also express more favorable attitudes toward housing assistance, here is what you can and cannot say.

You cannot sayKnowing someone who experiences homelessness causes people to support housing assistance.
You can sayRespondents reporting personal contact with homelessness also reported greater support for housing assistance.

Your survey found a relationship. It did not run an experiment. , and the difference is the difference between a careful researcher and an overreaching one.

Try it: what is wrong with each sentence?

Six sentences of the kind that turn up in student Results sections. Each one breaks exactly one of the four rules. Pick which, then check. Commit to an answer before you look.

2 · Choose the graph from the data, not because you like the graph

Students often do this backward. They think I want a pie chart. The question is not which chart you want. It is what relationship in my data am I trying to show? Then you pick the form that shows that relationship.

Here is the short version, and for most of you it is the only version you need.

You want to show…Use…Because
Differences between categoriesBar chartLength is easy for the eye to compare. Order the bars meaningfully, usually highest to lowest.
Responses across a scale, strongly disagree to strongly agreeBar chart, or a stacked barA 100% stacked bar lets a reader compare response patterns across several related questions at once.
Change over timeLine graphA line connects things, and the connection should mean something. Time moves left to right.
Exact values, or only a little informationTableThree numbers do not need a chart. A table respects the reader's time.
Themes, quotations, qualitative findingsText, or a thematic tableThese are findings, not quantities. Forcing them into a chart loses the thing that made them useful.
The distribution of one numerical variableHistogramAges, scores, counts. Most of you will not need one.
Whether two numerical variables move togetherScatterplotAlso probably not you, and remember what it cannot claim.
See it: comparing categories

Begin with a for each closed question, then decide what to show. Which factor do you believe contributes most to homelessness in our region? Housing costs 18, mental-health challenges 9, substance-use disorders 7, job loss 4, other 2.

What do we care about here? Comparison. So: a bar chart, with the bars ordered highest to lowest, so a reader sees the ranking without hunting for it.

And the pie chart. You knew we were getting here. Pie charts show parts of one whole, and they are not evil. They get hard to read as soon as you have more than a few slices. Compare these five: housing 38%, mental health 24%, substance use 19%, employment 12%, other 7%. A bar chart lets your eye rank those in about a second.

General advice: when in doubt, use a bar chart. Do not turn your paper into a pie-chart buffet. And no 3-D pie charts. We are not making slide decks in 1998.

A note on Likert questions, which most of you have. Local government should increase funding for permanent supportive housing: strongly disagree, disagree, neither, agree, strongly agree.

A simple bar chart works. A stacked bar works. With several related statements, a 100% stacked bar lets the reader compare patterns across questions. But remember: Those are ordered categories, not inches on a ruler, and you should not average them without a methodological reason.

One special survey problem: “select all that apply.” Ask Which factors do you believe contribute to homelessness? Select all that apply, and you might get housing affordability 72%, mental illness 61%, substance use 58%, low wages 49%.

Somebody will say those add up to more than 100%. Correct, because participants could pick more than one, and that is fine. Just label the chart so nobody has to reverse-engineer your method from it: Percentage of respondents selecting each factor; respondents could select more than one.

3 · How graphs lie

You can lie without falsifying a single number. Support for Policy A is 51%. Support for Policy B is 55%. Watch what happens when the vertical axis starts at 50 instead of at zero.

51%
55%
Axis starts at 50%. A four-point gap reads as a landslide.
51%
55%
Axis starts at zero. Same two numbers, accurate impression.
Now do it to your own numbers

Those two charts above are somebody else's. Open Chart Your Data, type in your actual tallies, and flip the misleading version toggle. Watch your own finding get exaggerated, then turn it off and leave it off.

That tool is also where you build the figure you will actually paste into P3, and it downloads as an .svg. It does not decide which chart you need; section 2 above did that. It runs on your own device and nothing you type into it is transmitted.

For a bar chart, the numerical axis should ordinarily begin at zero, because the physical length of the bar is what represents the quantity. Cut the bottom off and the lengths stop meaning what the eye assumes.

The ways a chart misleads without lying. Truncated axes. Inconsistent intervals. Giant icons standing for small differences. Three-dimensional effects. Showing only the convenient categories. Leaving out the missing data. Changing scales between two graphs you are asking a reader to compare side by side.

A technically correct number can be visually dishonest.

4 · Beautiful does not mean decorated

Two different ideas. Decoration asks what can I add? Design usually asks what can I remove?

A good chart almost certainly does not need a textured background, drop shadows, clip art, a rainbow palette, twelve fonts, six kinds of grid line, a giant legend, or a photograph behind the bars. The data are what a reader came for. Give them room.

Five things I want from every chart in your P3.

  1. 1
    A meaningful title. Not “Survey Results.” Try “Housing affordability was the most frequently identified contributor to homelessness.” The second one helps me read the graph.
  2. 2
    Labels I can understand. Do not make the reader decode Q17_RESP_3. Label it “Housing affordability.”
  3. 3
    Minimal visual clutter. Do I need this grid line? This border? A legend, if I could label the bars directly? If not, remove it.
  4. 4
    Color with a job. Maybe one bar is the finding; highlight that one. Everything does not need its own color. And never make color the only way to tell categories apart, because your chart still has to work for someone who cannot distinguish those colors.
  5. 5
    Context, so the figure stands on its own. What the data represent, the number of respondents, units, and any methodological note. For example: Figure 2. Factors respondents identified as contributing to homelessness (n = 42). Participants could select more than one response.

5 · The part your mixed-methods report actually needs

Your survey produces numbers. Most of you also have an open-ended question, something like Is there anything you wish people better understood about homelessness in this region? And you get answers like these.

Three open responses

“Rent is higher than people realize, especially if you're working for $12 an hour.”

“People talk like everybody living outside is addicted to drugs.”

“A lot of people are one medical emergency away from losing their housing.”

So what do you do with them? Two things you do not do. You do not drop 27 quotations into your Results section. And you do not read through them, pick the three you personally like best, and call that qualitative analysis.

Remember coding, from Week 6? The same principles apply here. Read across the responses, look for patterns, and develop categories.

Say your responses produce three themes.

Housing affordability. Participants discuss rent, deposits, wages, housing shortages.

Stereotypes about homelessness. Participants describe assumptions about addiction, employment, or personal responsibility.

Economic vulnerability. Participants discuss medical bills, layoffs, transportation, childcare, and other events that destabilize housing.

Now you have qualitative findings. Then select quotations because they are representative of the theme, not because they are the most dramatic. That is an ethical question too: one bizarre response from one participant should not be used to make your whole sample look bizarre.

Your numbers and your words have to talk to each other

This is what makes a report actually . Here is what it looks like when they do not.

Two methods sharing an apartmentHere are my survey results. [Three pages.] Here are some comments people wrote. [Two pages.]
IntegratedTwenty-nine of 42 respondents identified housing affordability. Open responses repeatedly connected rent to local wages, suggesting respondents understood affordability as a mismatch rather than simply as high rent.

So ask four questions of your open responses. Do they explain the quantitative pattern? Do they complicate it? Do they contradict it? Do they reveal something the closed-ended question could not capture?

Use a joint display

This can be very simple. Three columns, one row per finding. The third column is the one students leave out, and it is the one doing the analysis.

Quantitative findingQualitative findingWhat we learn by combining them
68% identified housing affordability as a major contributor Respondents repeatedly described rent rising faster than local wages Housing concerns were not abstract. Respondents connected affordability specifically to the relationship between wages and rent.
54% identified substance use as a contributor Several open responses objected to the stereotype that addiction explains all homelessness Participants may recognize substance use as one contributor while resisting a single-cause explanation.
43% supported more emergency shelter capacity Several respondents emphasized permanent housing instead of temporary shelter Support for intervention does not mean respondents agree about which intervention.

Read down that third column. What becomes visible only when you put both kinds of evidence together? That is mixed-methods analysis, and none of those three sentences could have been written from either column alone.

What goes in Results versus Discussion

This will save you time on Sunday, and it will save you again on the final report.

Results: what did you find?Housing affordability was the most commonly selected factor, identified by 29 of 42 respondents (69%). [Figure 1.] Open-ended responses reinforced this pattern, particularly through comments about the gap between wages and rental costs. [One representative quotation.]
Discussion: so what does it mean?Connect back to your research question. Compare the finding with your literature review. Consider explanations. State your . Talk about implications.

And do not make your graph do the discussion for you. A figure shows a pattern; only your prose can say what it means.

6 · A special note for the focus-group project

You are not required to have a graph.

If six people participated, I do not want 33.3% of participants believed… That is two people. Just tell me: Two participants expressed… Or, if the frequency is not the point, Housing affordability emerged as one of the strongest themes in the discussion. Then show the evidence.

More importantly, the interaction itself is data. Your Results section can be organized around themes, and each theme does three things: describe the pattern, give a representative quotation, then show where another participant expanded, complicated, or disagreed with it.

What that looks like

Theme 2: Tension between personal responsibility and structural explanation. One participant initially emphasized individual choices. Another challenged that explanation by discussing wages and rent. The conversation shifted, and a third participant returned to the first position with a qualification.

That exchange is one of your findings. You cannot put it in a pie chart, and you should not try.

If you do want a display, you have three options, and the first one is completely acceptable.

Option 1: no visualization. Strong thematic subheadings and well-chosen quotations.

Option 2: a thematic table. A legitimate data display, and often the right one.

Option 3: a thematic map, but only if your themes actually relate to one another and the visual communicates that relationship. Housing cost leads to housing instability leads to homelessness, with a health crisis, a job loss, or a transportation failure interacting with that pathway. Do not make a diagram because everybody else has a graph.

And demographics from six participants? Probably a table, not a graph. Four undergraduates, two graduate students. But ask first whether the demographic information is doing any work at all, and then think about privacy. With a sample this small, combining characteristics can accidentally identify someone. If there is exactly one 52-year-old graduate student who works at a local shelter, you have practically named her.

Qualitative research does not owe the reader every demographic detail you collected.

7 · The final rule

Keep four words in mind as you build your Results section.

Show

Show me the pattern with a visualization, when a visualization actually helps.

Tell

Tell me the finding in prose. Never make your reader interpret the graph alone.

Explain

Explain with the qualitative evidence that gives the numbers depth, complexity, or contradiction.

Connect

Connect the quantitative and qualitative findings to each other.

Numbers without people become abstraction. Stories without patterns become anecdote. Mixed-methods research gives you both, and the point is not to decide which one is telling the truth. The point is to ask: what can I understand by seeing them together that I could not understand from either one alone?

When your figures exist, audit them. There is a nine-point ethics audit in the Week 14 module, built to run on a finished figure: sample size visible, denominators right, axis at zero, precision matched to your N, title claiming only what the sample allows, color not carrying the meaning alone, and nothing decorative left in. Run it on every figure before the final report, and run it on your P3 figures this weekend if you have time.

Primary vs. secondary research

Secondary research means analyzing sources other people produced (your literature review, your food-label analysis). Primary research means generating and analyzing new data yourself. Charting your own survey results is primary analysis: nobody has seen these findings before you, because you made them.

Frequency table

A simple count of how many people chose each answer. If 14 of 42 respondents picked "Sometimes," that's a frequency. It turns a column of individual responses into countable totals you can compare and chart, the first step of any quantitative analysis.

Averaging a Likert scale

People often report "the average agreement was 3.8 of 5," and it's common enough to be acceptable. Just know the caveat: a Likert scale is ordered (agree is more than neutral) but the steps aren't guaranteed to be evenly spaced, so an average can smooth over a split. Showing the full distribution in a bar chart usually tells a truer story.

Correlation vs. causation

Two things moving together doesn't mean one caused the other. If label-readers also happen to exercise more, reading labels didn't necessarily cause the exercise, some third thing (health-consciousness) might drive both. Report what's associated in your data, and resist writing that one thing made another happen unless you truly tested it.

Stating your limitations

Every study has them, and saying so makes you more credible. A small or convenience sample is a limitation: "This sample of 34 mostly-student respondents can't represent all residents, so these findings are suggestive." Stating your limits is what separates a careful researcher from someone overselling their data.

Mixed methods

Combining numbers and words so each covers the other's blind spot. Your closed questions give the what (percentages, charts); your open responses and any interviews give the why (quotes, reasons). Setting a participant's own words next to the matching statistic is the move that makes a quantitative write-up feel human and convincing.
