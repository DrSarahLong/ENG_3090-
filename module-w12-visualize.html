Analyzing & Visualizing Your Data

This is the payoff. The responses you gathered become findings, charts, and, by Sunday, your P3. Budget about ninety minutes, plus time to write it up.

The moment your study answers its own question. All semester you found a gap, framed a question, built an instrument, and gathered data. Today those responses finally talk back. As the , you're the first person on earth to see what your data says. Your job now is to read it honestly and show it clearly.

Step 1: turn responses into counts

Open your exported spreadsheet. For each question, count how many people chose each answer, that's a , and it's the foundation of everything else. You can tally by hand for a small survey, or let the sheet do it: counts matching answers automatically. Do this for every closed question before you chart anything.

Step 2: describe it, simply

You don't need advanced statistics. For a class project, three carry most of the weight:

  • Count (N): how many people answered. Report it every time.
  • Percentage: what share picked each answer. Easier to compare than raw counts.
  • Mode: the most common answer. For truly numeric data (age, price), you can add a .

A note on : reporting "the average was 3.8 out of 5" is common, but it's a shortcut worth flagging, since the gap between "agree" and "strongly agree" isn't guaranteed to be the same size as the gap between "neutral" and "agree." Showing the full distribution in a bar chart is usually more honest.

Step 3: pick the chart that fits the data

The should match what you're showing, not just what looks cool:

  • Bar chart: comparing answer choices or categories. The workhorse, and almost always a safe choice.
  • Pie chart: parts of a single whole that add to 100%, and only with a few slices. Skip it if you have many categories.
  • Line chart: change over time (less common for a one-shot survey).
  • Grouped or stacked bars: comparing groups, do younger and older shoppers answer differently?

Build yours in the tool from class. Load your own counts, try bar versus pie, and download the chart straight into your report.

Step 4: don't be the misleading label

You've spent a whole semester on how food labels can mislead. A chart can do the exact same thing, and usually by accident. The honesty rules:

  • Start bars at zero. A turns a 4-point gap into a fake landslide.
  • Label everything: axes, units, categories, and your N.
  • Don't cherry-pick. Show all the answer choices, not just the flattering ones.
  • Match the visual to the size. Don't make a 3-vote difference look like a movement.

If your reader would walk away believing something your data doesn't actually support, the chart is lying, even if every number in it is true.

Step 5: read the results honestly

Two traps catch new researchers. First, : if label-readers also eat more vegetables, reading labels didn't necessarily cause it. Second, a small sample is suggestive, not proof. Say what you found, then say plainly what your data can and can't claim. Naming your makes you more trustworthy, not less.

Step 6: bring the words back in

Your numbers show the what. Your open-ended responses show the why. Pull one or two real quotes from participants and set them beside the relevant chart. That pairing, a number plus a voice, is the heart of a write-up, and it's what turns a stack of percentages into an argument.

✍ On your own

Look at your finished chart and ask: if a stranger saw only this, with no explanation, what would they conclude? If that conclusion is truer than your data supports, fix the chart. If it's exactly right, you've done the job.

Primary vs. secondary research

Secondary research means analyzing sources other people produced (your literature review, your food-label analysis). Primary research means generating and analyzing new data yourself. Charting your own survey results is primary analysis: nobody has seen these findings before you, because you made them.

Frequency table

A simple count of how many people chose each answer. If 14 of 42 respondents picked "Sometimes," that's a frequency. It turns a column of individual responses into countable totals you can compare and chart, the first step of any quantitative analysis.

COUNTIF

A spreadsheet formula that counts cells matching a condition. In Google Sheets, =COUNTIF(B2:B200,"Often") tells you how many people answered "Often." Run it once per answer choice and you've built your frequency table in seconds, no hand-tallying, no miscounts.

Descriptive statistics

Plain numbers that summarize your data, no advanced math. The ones you'll use: the count (N), the percentage for each answer, and the mode (most common answer). For numeric data you can add a mean or median. You do not need regression or p-values for this project.

Mean vs. median

The mean is the average (add them up, divide by how many). The median is the middle value when you line them up. Use them for genuinely numeric answers like age or price. The median is sturdier when a few extreme values would drag the average around, one shopper who spends $600 a week shouldn't define "typical."

Averaging a Likert scale

People often report "the average agreement was 3.8 of 5," and it's common enough to be acceptable. Just know the caveat: a Likert scale is ordered (agree is more than neutral) but the steps aren't guaranteed to be evenly spaced, so an average can smooth over a split. Showing the full distribution in a bar chart usually tells a truer story.

Match the chart to the data

Bar: comparing categories (the safe default). Pie: parts of one whole adding to 100%, with only a few slices. Line: change over time. Grouped/stacked bars: comparing groups against each other. When in doubt, a clean bar chart is rarely wrong.

The truncated-axis trick

The most common way charts mislead: starting the vertical axis near the data instead of at zero, so small differences look huge. A move from 48% to 52% gets drawn as a landslide. Start your bars at zero and you can't make this mistake. The class tool has a toggle that shows the trick on your own data.

Correlation vs. causation

Two things moving together doesn't mean one caused the other. If label-readers also happen to exercise more, reading labels didn't necessarily cause the exercise, some third thing (health-consciousness) might drive both. Report what's associated in your data, and resist writing that one thing made another happen unless you truly tested it.

Naming your limitations

Every study has them, and saying so out loud makes you more credible. A small or convenience sample is a limitation: "This sample of 34 mostly-student shoppers can't represent all consumers, so these findings are suggestive." Stating your limits is what separates a careful researcher from someone overselling their data.

Mixed methods

Combining numbers and words so each covers the other's blind spot. Your closed questions give the what (percentages, charts); your open responses and any interviews give the why (quotes, reasons). Setting a participant's own words next to the matching statistic is the move that makes a quantitative write-up feel human and convincing.
