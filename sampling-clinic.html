Who Gets Asked?

You're about to collect real data. Who you ask decides what you'll find, and who you leave out decides what you'll miss. Eight recruiting choices below. For each, decide: does it represent your topic fairly, is it too narrow, or does it cross a line?

Two questions run under all of these. Is this ? Does my fairly stand in for the people my question is about, or did I quietly stack it? And our lens: whose voices are in, and whose are missing? A sample that only hears from people like you answers a smaller question than you think.

On your own: make your call, then open the verdict. We'll argue the close ones as a class.

Reveal the verdict

Scenario 8

A student who wants to prove that "natural" labels are misleading shares the survey only in an online group for people who already call out greenwashing.

Reveal the verdict
Too narrow She recruited the choir. A group that already distrusts labels will hand her the answer she wanted, which is aimed straight at her hypothesis. Real research tries to disprove itself: go ask people who've never thought about greenwashing at all.
Sampling to confirm, not to find out

The through-line: before you send that link, ask three things. Do the people I'm reaching actually represent who my question is about? Whose voices am I missing, and how do I reach them? Am I collecting to find out, or to confirm what I already believe? A study is only as trustworthy as the sample behind it, and you're the one who builds that sample.

Sample vs. population

Your population is everyone your question is really about (say, "grocery shoppers"). You can't ask all of them, so you ask a sample, a smaller slice. The whole game is choosing a slice that fairly stands in for the whole. A skewed sample answers a narrower question than the one you meant to ask.

Representative sample

A sample that mirrors the larger group you care about, so what you find in the sample is a fair guess about the population. Big professional studies use random sampling to get there. Class projects rarely can, so you aim for reasonable spread instead: deliberately reach several different kinds of people, and be honest in your write-up about who you did and didn't reach.

Convenience sample

Recruiting whoever is easiest to reach, your friends, your class, people walking by. It's what most class projects use, and that's okay, as long as you name its limit: the people who are easy for you to reach tend to resemble you, which quietly narrows your findings. Counter it by reaching into a few groups that aren't your usual circle.

Selection bias

When where or how you recruit decides the answer before anyone responds. Survey only farmers'-market shoppers about local food and they'll love it, not because everyone does, but because you only asked people who already opted in. The fix is to recruit in places that don't pre-select for your expected answer.

Cherry-picking participants

Choosing who to ask based on who you think will give you the "right" answer (approaching only people who "look healthy," skipping people who seem skeptical). It stacks the deck with your own bias. If you recruit in person, use a simple rule, ask everyone or every nth person, so you're not the one deciding who counts.

Incentives

A small reward (a gift-card drawing, a few dollars) to thank people and lift your response rate. Perfectly ethical when it's modest and disclosed. It becomes a problem only if it's big enough to pressure someone into taking part they'd otherwise refuse, especially people who could really use the money. Keep it small, keep it honest.

Response rate

The share of people you invited who actually responded. Low response rates are normal, and they matter: if only the most passionate people bother to answer, your results tilt toward strong opinions. You lift it with clear invitations, a short survey, easy access (a link or QR code), a friendly reminder, and sometimes a small incentive.

Coercion

Any pressure that makes "no" feel unsafe or costly, a boss telling staff to take your survey, doing it on paid time, a professor surveying their own students for a grade. It breaks the voluntary part of informed consent (Week 9). Recruit where people can freely opt in or out, with no one in power leaning on them.

Sample size

How many responses you gather. Too few and you can't tell a real pattern from random noise, or chart anything convincingly. There's no magic number, but for a class survey, aim for a few dozen and more if you can. Interviews work differently: five to eight rich conversations can be plenty, because depth, not count, is the point. Whatever you end with, report it honestly.
