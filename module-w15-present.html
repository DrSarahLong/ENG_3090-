ENG 3090 · Week 15 · Thursday Module

Present Your Research

The last step. You stand up and tell us what you found, in about five minutes. Today you build that talk.

Arrive. Open your Research Notebook in another tab and take a breath. This is the last module of the semester. You have a finished study behind you, a real one you designed, ran, analyzed, and wrote up. Today's job is to turn it into a five-minute talk you can stand up and give.

Your report is fifteen pages a reader moves through slowly. A talk is five minutes an audience hears once, live. Different medium, different rules, and building yours is today's work.

1 · You're the authority in the room

Nobody else collected your data, read your texts, or talked to your participants. For your five minutes, you know more about your question than anyone here, including me. A presentation is just you, the , telling us what you learned. Walk in believing that, because it's true.

2 · A talk is not your paper read aloud

Your report is built for a reader who can slow down, back up, and reread. Your talk is built for an audience who gets one pass, at your pace, out loud. You can't say everything in five minutes, so don't try. A good talk is a compressed built to land main point.

3 · The six beats

Most strong research talks move through the same six beats. Think of them as the spine of your five minutes.

  • 1
    Hook. Open so the room cares: a striking number, a question, a quick real moment. Not "Hi, my project is about…"
  • 2
    The question & why it matters. What you set out to find, and the gap that made it worth finding.
  • 3
    What you did. Your method, in a sentence or two. Enough that they trust it, not every detail.
  • 4
    What you found. One or two key findings, with your single best on screen.
  • 5
    So what. Your recommendation, the "now what." This is where your work earns its point.
  • 6
    Close. One memorable last line, then "thank you." End clean; don't trail off.
See it — a hook, from our example

Instead of opening with "My project looked at egg-carton labels," our running example talk might open: "Nine cartons sat on one shelf. Every one said natural. Not one of them meant the same thing." A number, a small real moment, and the room leans in before you've even said your question.

Eggs are our running example, not your topic. Watch the move, then build your own hook from your own finding.

4 · Slides that help, not hurt

Slides are a backdrop, not your script. Keep them light and let your voice carry the talk.

  • Few words per slide. Your audience can read or listen, not both. If a slide needs a paragraph, it's a handout, not a slide.
  • One strong , big, with its caption. The chart you made in Week 12 is perfect here. Let it do the talking for your Results.
  • Readable from the back. Large type, high contrast, no tiny tables.

A handful of clean slides beats a dense deck every time.

5 · Talk, don't read

Reading a script word for word drains the life out of a talk and loses the room. Use a few as reminders, look up, and talk to us like people. Know your first line and last line cold so you start and end strong even if the middle wobbles. Slow down (it always feels faster to you than to us), and breathe.

6 · Practice out loud and time it

Rehearse the whole talk , on a clock, at least twice. It's the only way to know your true length (talks always run long) and to smooth the rough patches before you're in front of anyone. If you're over time, cut a detail, not your takeaway. Practicing in your head does not count.

Your turn

Say your talk out loud once, right now, start to finish, on a timer. Out loud, not in your head. In Entry 15, note where you ran long, where you stumbled, and your actual time. That one run will teach you more than another hour of tweaking slides.

7 · The questions after

A short follows each talk. You can prepare for it: almost every research talk gets some version of "what surprised you?", "what would you do differently?", and "what are the limitations?" Have a genuine answer ready for each. And "that's a good question, I'm not sure, but here's my guess" is a completely respectable answer. You don't have to know everything.

You're the investigator

All semester you moved from secondary research (reading what others made) to primary research (making your own knowledge). Presenting is the last move: you stand in front of a room as the person who produced these findings. That authority is real, you did the work, so own it.

A talk is a compressed IMRAD

Your report's structure runs underneath the talk, just shorter. Hook and question do the work of the Introduction; "what you did" is Methods; "what you found" is Results; "so what" is Discussion. Same arc, about five minutes, one clear point.

One takeaway

An audience forgets nearly everything in a short talk except, if you're lucky, one idea. So choose that idea deliberately and say it twice, near the start and again at the close. If the room walks out remembering only your takeaway, you succeeded. Trying to deliver everything usually delivers nothing.

Show one strong visual

Put your single best chart or table on a slide, large, with its caption ("Figure 1. …, N = 42"). A clear visual lands in three seconds what a paragraph of talking can't. Avoid text-heavy slides; the audience can't read and listen at once. One figure they grasp beats five bullets they don't.

Talk, don't read

Reading a script flattens your voice and loses eye contact. Keep a few notes as reminders and speak to the audience. You know this material better than anyone in the room; trust that. Talking to people beats a perfect paragraph read to the floor.

Practice out loud and time it

Rehearse the whole thing aloud at least twice, on a clock. It's the only way to learn your real length (talks always run long) and to smooth the rough spots before you're in front of people. Practicing silently doesn't count; your mouth needs the reps. Finish comfortably under your limit.

Handling questions

The Q&A isn't a trap; people ask because they're interested. Prepare for the usual three, what surprised you, what you'd do differently, what your limitations are, and you'll field most of what comes. If you don't know, say so and offer your best guess. Confident uncertainty reads far better than bluffing.
