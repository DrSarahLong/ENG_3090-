The Question Clinic

A survey is only as good as its questions. A bad question quietly writes its own answer, and no analysis later can fix the data it gave you. Eight questions below, all about food labels. For each, decide: is it clean, or does it need work?

The rule of the room: garbage in, garbage out. Watch for the six usual suspects, , , , , , and an . A clean question asks one clear thing, in plain words, without tipping its hand.

On your own: make your call, then open the verdict to see the flaw named and a rewrite. We'll argue the close ones as a class.

Question 1

"Don't you agree that misleading 'natural' labels are a problem for honest shoppers?"

Reveal the verdict
Needs workLeading
It hands the respondent the answer. "Don't you agree," "misleading," and "honest shoppers" all push toward yes. You'll get agreement you manufactured, not what people actually think. A inflates your own conclusion.
Rewrite: "How much do you agree or disagree that 'natural' labels are misleading?" with a balanced agree-to-disagree scale.

Question 2

"How satisfied are you with the accuracy and the pricing of organic products?"

Reveal the verdict
Needs workDouble-barreled
It asks about two different things, accuracy and price, in one question. Someone who trusts the labels but hates the prices has no honest way to answer, and you can't tell which half their answer is about. A gives you data you can't interpret.
Rewrite: split it. One question on label accuracy, a separate one on price.

Question 3

"When you stopped buying products with fake sustainability claims, what made you switch?"

Reveal the verdict
Needs workLoaded
It smuggles in assumptions: that the person stopped buying these products, and that the claims are "fake." Someone who never stopped, or who trusts the claims, is stuck. A assumes facts not yet established.
Rewrite: ask first, "Have you changed what you buy because of sustainability labels?" Then, only for those who say yes, "What changed your mind?"

Question 4

"Do you buy sustainable food?"

Reveal the verdict
Needs workVague
"Sustainable" means ten different things to ten people, and "do you buy" flattens "once" and "every week" into the same yes. Two people can answer the same way and mean nothing alike. A gives you numbers that don't actually count anything.
Rewrite: define the term and ask frequency: "In a typical month, how often do you choose a product because it's labeled 'organic' or 'regenerative'? (Never / Rarely / Sometimes / Often / Always)"

Question 5

"To what extent do eco-labels mitigate information asymmetry at the point of purchase?"

Reveal the verdict
Needs workJargon
"Information asymmetry" and "at the point of purchase" belong in your paper, not your survey. If a respondent has to decode the question, some will guess and some will quit. A loses you both answers and people.
Rewrite: "When you're in the store, how much do labels like 'organic' or 'local' help you know what you're really buying?"

Question 6

"How would you rate 'local' labels?  Excellent  ·  Very good  ·  Good  ·  Fair"

Reveal the verdict
Needs workUnbalanced scale
Every option is positive. There's no "poor," no "terrible," nowhere for a skeptic to land, so the results will look rosier than reality. An bakes the bias right into the answer choices.
Rewrite: a balanced with a neutral middle: Very negative · Negative · Neutral · Positive · Very positive.

Question 7

"Please explain in detail every factor that influences all of your grocery decisions."

Reveal the verdict
Needs workToo broad
"Every factor," "all of your decisions," "in detail," on an question, is a wall most people won't climb. You'll get one-word answers or blanks, and what you do get you can't count. Save open questions for one sharp thing you want in the participant's own words.
Rewrite: narrow it: "What's the one thing that most makes you trust, or distrust, a sustainability label?" Or make it a closed question with set options.

Question 8

"How often do you look for a 'certified organic' seal when buying produce?  Never  ·  Rarely  ·  Sometimes  ·  Often  ·  Always"

Reveal the verdict
Clean
One clear idea (looking for a specific seal), plain words, a specific product (produce), and a balanced, labeled that runs from never to always. Everyone reads it the same way, and the answers are . This is the model to aim for.

Before a question goes in your survey, run it past four checks. Does it ask exactly one thing? Would someone who disagrees with me feel free to say so? Could two people read it two different ways? Can I actually count the answers? If a question fails one, fix it now. It's a lot cheaper than discovering it after fifty people have answered.

Leading question

A question worded so it nudges the respondent toward a particular answer, often with loaded phrasing or a built-in "right" side ("Don't you agree that…"). It inflates whatever you were hoping to find. The fix is neutral wording and a balanced set of answer choices, so agreeing and disagreeing feel equally allowed.

Double-barreled question

One question that actually asks about two things at once ("the accuracy and the price"). A single answer can't cleanly cover both, so you can't tell what the response means. If you see an "and" or an "or" joining two ideas you'd want to measure separately, split it into two questions.

Loaded question

A question that assumes something the respondent hasn't agreed to yet ("When did you stop…?" assumes they stopped). Anyone for whom the assumption is false has no honest way to answer. Ask the prior question first, then branch: only ask the follow-up of the people the assumption actually fits.

Vague question

A question built on undefined words ("sustainable," "often," "healthy") that different people read differently. Two respondents can give the same answer and mean completely different things, which makes the data meaningless. Define your terms in the question, or replace a fuzzy word with a concrete, countable one.

Jargon

Technical or academic language ("information asymmetry," "point of purchase") that belongs in your write-up, not in front of participants. If people have to decode the question, some guess and some quit, and both wreck your data. Write survey questions at the reading level of the person answering, not the person grading.

Balanced response scale

The answer choices should offer as much room to be negative as to be positive. A scale that runs "Excellent · Very good · Good · Fair" has no bottom end, so it collects praise by design. A balanced scale (very negative → neutral → very positive) lets the real distribution show up instead of tilting it for you.

Likert scale

The classic agree-to-disagree (or never-to-always) scale, named for psychologist Rensis Likert. Use an odd number of points so there's a genuine neutral middle, label every point (not just the ends), and keep the two sides balanced. It turns an opinion into a number you can average, chart, and compare, which is exactly what you'll need in Week 12.

Closed vs. open questions

Closed questions give set answer choices (multiple choice, a scale), so responses are countable and easy to chart. Open questions let people answer in their own words, which is richer but slow to analyze. For P3 you want mostly closed questions (you have to count and visualize the results) plus a few open ones to catch quotes and surprises.
