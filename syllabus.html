Course description

This course prepares you to become a responsible, well-informed researcher in rhetoric and technical writing. Over the semester you'll design and carry out your own mixed-methods study on a single topic of your choosing and write it up as a professional IMRAD report, a portfolio-quality artifact you can show an employer or a grad program. Along the way we cover the historical and definitional links between rhetoric and technical writing, philosophical orientations to research, and real research methods, both critical/qualitative and empirical/quantitative.

Our lens: social justice. We study these methods through a social justice frame, because technical writing is never neutral. The documents, data, and interfaces we build decide who gets access, who carries risk, and whose story gets told. You'll choose a semester topic that lives inside that frame, and we'll treat research as a tool for noticing and addressing inequity, not just describing the world.

Course objectives

General education: critical thinking (investigate problems from multiple viewpoints using credible sources); creative thinking (extend ideas across boundaries); effective communication (tailor context, audience, form, purpose); evidence-based arguments (integrate credible sources).

Course-specific: define and apply RTW research methods; conduct responsible, ethical research; understand research design as an iterative process; build portfolio-worthy artifacts.

How this course works

Each week has two distinct parts:

Tuesday (in class, Boone): a short, hands-on concept session. I introduce the week's key idea through a live worked example and a guided Research Notebook you fill in as we go, then we practice it together in an in-class exercise. This is where the concept lands and you can ask questions live.

Thursday (remote via Zoom): we still meet, just online. I open our Zoom Room with a short orientation to the day's module, then you work through it on your own while I stay on Zoom for questions; we come back together at the end for anything unresolved. You can pop in and out as you need, and I expect you to join. The module reinforces Tuesday's concept and scaffolds your next deliverable, never busywork.

The first two weeks are different. We meet on campus both days for Weeks 1 and 2. That's where we get grounded, you choose the topic you'll carry all semester, and I walk you through exactly how the Thursday Zoom work days run. Starting Week 3, we shift to the regular rhythm.

The weekly work & your Research Notebook (20%)

Each week's module goes into your Research Notebook, the running digital notebook you build all semester. Modules are completion only: there are no wrong answers; the point is that you do the thinking, because that's what sets you up to succeed on the projects. You turn the notebook in just twice, once at midterm and once at the end of term. You type in the browser, your work saves in your browser on your own computer as you go, and at each check-in you hit Download everything and submit it to AsULearn (as a PDF or a Google Doc, your choice). Do each module as we go: the work compounds, and a crammed notebook shows.

The major project

You'll choose one research topic early and carry it through the whole semester as an individual mixed-methods study. It builds in four stacked deliverables that assemble into your final report. The weekly modules keep things fresh by practicing each skill on wildly different examples, but your study stays on one topic.

#ProjectWeightIn short
P1Literature Review15%Synthesize 10+ credible sources on your topic; find the gap your study will address. 2+ single-spaced pp.
P2Qualitative / Discourse Analysis15%Design and run a discourse or content analysis; report the meaning and power your data reveal. 3+ pp.
P3Quantitative Analysis15%Design an instrument, collect data, analyze and visualize it. 2+ pp plus visuals.
FinalIMRAD Report35%Integrate all three into a professional IMRAD report with actionable recommendations. 15–20 sources.
—Research Notebook (completion)20%Your weekly module work, turned in at midterm and end of term.

Full rubrics and examples for each project live on the Major Project Rubrics page. Where and how to submit each piece is on the What's Due & Where guide, in short: P1, P2, and P3 come in as Google Docs (so you get inline comments), and the final report is a PDF.

Teaching approach

I'm a "guide on the side" more than a "sage on the stage," but on Tuesdays I will teach the core concept directly, and the modules lead you through the doing. I design curriculum that engages different learning styles, choose readings that aren't obtuse, and build assignments that help you apply theory. What I won't do is hold your hand or police your attendance. Success in this course, however you define it, is your responsibility. I'll do everything in my power to support students who take that seriously.

Late project work is not accepted except under extraordinary, documented circumstances. Talk to me early, not after.

Using AI is not plagiarism in this class, but I expect you to use it appropriately and ethically. See these guidelines for the ethical use of generative AI (Santa Clara University). Learning to use it well is part of the professional skill set.
