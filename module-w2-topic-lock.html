Meet the Modules, and Lock Your Topic

Your first module. We're doing this one together in class, so you'll always know how they work.

Arrive. Open your Research Notebook in another tab and take a breath. On Tuesday you built and pressure-tested your research question. Today you turn it into a locked topic: the real texts you'll analyze, the people you could ask, and the justice angle that makes it worth doing. That's what turns a question into a semester-long project.

The question was the hard, careful half, and you've done it. A topic you can actually research needs three more things, and building them is today's work.

1 · How these modules work

  • Read a little, do a little. Every Thursday you'll work through one of these on your own time. Short teaching, then something you actually write.
  • Your writing lands in your Research Notebook, which saves on your own computer. Nothing is transmitted anywhere.
  • Completion only. No grade on the writing itself, just that you did the thinking.
  • You only turn the notebook in twice all semester: at midterm and at the end. Not every week.

But do them every week anyway. Two reasons. First, each module builds a piece of your major project, so skipping them means building the whole thing from scratch later, under deadline. Second, together they are worth a considerable chunk of your grade (a full fifth). Cramming them all right before a check-in defeats the point: this way of learning only works when the reps are spread out.

3 · What makes a topic "lockable"

A topic locks when it gives you all four of these. You built the fourth on Tuesday, so today is about the other three.

  • 1
    Texts you can analyze. Anything you can get your hands on, labels, manuals, reports, ads, posts, policies, signage.
  • 2
    People you can ask. A group you can realistically survey or interview.
  • 3
    A justice angle. Who holds power here, who's served, and whose voice is missing?
  • 4
    A strong — done Tuesday. ✔

Boxes 1, 2, and 3 — texts, people, and a justice angle — are today's work. Take each in turn.

4 · Box 1 — texts you can analyze

Half your evidence is : real documents made of language that you gather and read closely. In this field almost anything counts, labels and packaging, manuals and instructions, policies and forms, ads, web pages, posts, signage, reports.

See it — texts hiding in one topic

The egg-carton topic isn't one text, it's a stack: the front-of-carton claims, the fine print, the certification seals, the store's shelf signs, even the brand's website. Each one is a text you could analyze.

5 · Box 2 — people you can ask

The other half of your evidence is : a group you could reach with a short survey or a few interviews. It doesn't have to be everyone. It has to be reachable, this semester, by you.

See it

For the egg-carton topic: shoppers leaving that one grocery, or students in a dining hall who buy their own groceries. Not "all consumers", a specific group you could actually stand near and ask.

Your turn

In Entry 2b, name one group you could realistically ask, and where you'd find them. We design the actual survey or interview in Week 10, so a reachable group is all you need today.

6 · Box 3 — the justice angle

Technical writing is never neutral. Every document decides who can understand it, who it leaves out, and who carries the risk when it fails. Your is three questions you keep asking: who holds power here, who is served, and whose voice is missing?

Read the real thing · 6 min

Technical Communication, Social Justice, and Collective Access — an open-textbook chapter on how the documents we design include or exclude people, and why access itself is a justice issue.

See it — in three different topics

Egg cartons: power sits with the brands writing the claims and the agencies loosely policing them; served are shoppers who can already decode a label; missing are those who overpay for a word like "natural" that means little, and small farms priced out of certification.

A city's online permit form: power is the city's web team; served are people with broadband, English, and time; missing are phone-only users, non-English speakers, and anyone the form quietly defeats.

A workplace safety manual: power is the employer; served are experienced, English-fluent workers; missing are new, temporary, or non-English-speaking workers, the ones who most need it to be clear.

Your turn

In Entry 2b, answer the three for your topic: who holds power, who's served, whose voice is missing? A rough first pass is fine, you'll sharpen it all semester.

Research question

The specific, answerable question your whole study is built to answer. Not a topic (food labels) and not an opinion (are big companies evil), but a question you could actually settle with data you're able to collect.

A good one tells you what you'd go measure, ask, or count.

Discourse (and analyzing it)

Discourse just means language in use: the actual words, framing, and design of real texts like labels, ads, manuals, and posts. Studying it closely, term by term and choice by choice, is discourse or content analysis, and it's a core method of this course.

When you study how a carton says "farm fresh," you're doing exactly that, reading how language does work in the world.

Who you ask: population vs. sample

Your population is everyone your question is really about (say, grocery shoppers). Your sample is the reachable slice you actually ask (shoppers at one store, one afternoon).

You can't reach a whole population, and you don't need to. A thoughtful sample is how real studies work. We design yours in Week 10.

The social-justice lens

Technical writing is never neutral. Every document, form, chart, or interface makes choices about who can understand it, who it leaves out, and who carries the risk when it fails.

The lens is just three questions we keep asking while we research and write: who holds power here? who's served? whose voice is missing? Not to score points, to notice that design has real consequences for real people, and to do something about it.
