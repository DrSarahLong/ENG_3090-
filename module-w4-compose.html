Enter the Conversation

You planned your review on Tuesday. Today you write it, and you leave with a real paragraph of P1 drafted.

Arrive. Open your Research Notebook in another tab and take a breath. On Tuesday you built your review skeleton in Entry 4a: the themes you found in your sources and which source sits under each. Today you turn one of those themes into a paragraph that puts your sources in conversation, the move that separates a literature review from a stack of summaries.

You've gathered the voices and sorted them into themes. Today you make them talk to each other on the page, and then step in yourself.

1 · Scholarship is a dinner party

Scholarship is a dinner party that started long before you walked in. People have been arguing about your topic for years. You don't burst through the door and shout your opinion over the appetizers.

You find a seat. You listen. You figure out who's saying what, and who disagrees. Then you join in a way that shows you were paying attention: "Smith and Jones have been making the case for X; Lee isn't convinced; here's what I'd add." called this the "unending conversation" in a parlor, back in 1941.

Your literature review is you earning your seat at the table, before you say your own piece. The rest of this module is the three moves that get you into the conversation.

2 · Move one: report what they say, fairly

Don't drop a quote and run. Frame it: who is talking, and what are they doing with the claim? A plus a precise verb does the work. A vague "says" tells your reader nothing about whether the source proved it, guessed it, or warned against it.

Verbs that carry meaning: argues, finds, shows, reports, observes, suggests, contends, warns, cautions, concludes. ("Says" is fine in moderation, but the others tell your reader more.)
See it — in our example

Smith (2021) argues that shoppers routinely misread "natural."

The food-label study is our running example all semester, not your topic. Watch the move, then run it on a source from your own set.

Your turn

In Entry 4b, take one source under your first theme and write a single sentence that frames it: a signal phrase, the author and year, and a verb that says what they actually did with the claim.

3 · Move two: weave, don't list

The difference between a summary and a is putting voices in the same breath. Group the sources that agree, and name the tension where they don't. A review that goes "Smith says… Jones says… Lee says…" is a list. A review that says "two studies agree, but a third complicates it" is a conversation.

See it — list vs. weave

List: Smith (2021) found shoppers misread "natural." Jones (2020) found the same about "farm fresh." Lee (2022) found some shoppers weren't fooled.

Weave: Several studies (Smith 2021; Jones 2020) find that vague terms mislead shoppers, though Lee (2022) cautions that some see right through them.

4 · Move three: add your voice, turn to the gap

Once the conversation is on the page, step in. This is where your review stops reporting and starts pointing at your own study: the question nobody at the table has answered yet.

See it

What this conversation has not addressed is ___, which is where my study enters.

Your turn

In Entry 4b, add one sentence that names the gap your first theme leaves open, and connects it to your research question.

5 · All three moves, one paragraph

Here is the whole thing assembled, so you can see the seams. This is our food-label example; yours will use your own sources and your own gap.

"Research on food labeling consistently finds that shoppers misread vague terms: Smith (2021) shows that most read 'natural' as 'organic,' and Jones (2020) reports the same confusion around 'farm fresh.' This is less a failure of attention than of design. Regulators leave 'natural' almost undefined (FDA, 2022), and industry groups have fought to keep it that way (Lee, 2023). What this conversation has not asked is what that engineered confusion costs the small, honest producer, the very question this study takes up."

Notice: report (Smith shows, Jones reports), weave (two voices in one sentence, then a second pair), turn to the gap (what nobody asked). You never wrote "Source 1 says."

6 · Write your first theme

Open your Research Notebook and finish Entry 4b: take one full theme from your skeleton and write it as a single paragraph, using all three moves in order. Then come back and do your other themes the same way.

Signal phrase

A few words that name who is talking and frame their point before you quote or paraphrase: "Smith argues…," "According to Jones…," "As the FDA notes…." It hands your reader the source's identity and your take on it in one move, so a quote never lands out of nowhere.

Without: "Natural" is misleading (Smith, 2021).  With: Smith (2021) argues that "natural" misleads shoppers.

Synthesis

Combining several sources into a single point (what they reveal together) instead of summarizing them one at a time. Synthesis is the whole job of a literature review.

List: "Smith says… Jones says… Lee says…"  Synthesis: "Two studies agree that vague terms mislead shoppers (Smith 2021; Jones 2020), but Lee (2022) complicates it."

Who is Kenneth Burke?

An American literary theorist and rhetorician (1897–1993). In 1941 he wrote his famous "parlor" image: you show up at a party where a heated conversation is already going. Nobody stops to catch you up. You listen a while, get the drift, and then put in your oar. Scholarship works exactly like that, and so does your literature review.
