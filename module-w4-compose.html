Enter the Conversation

You've planned your review (Entry 4a). Now you write it. By the end of this one you'll have drafted a real paragraph of P1. Budget about 45 minutes.

Step 1: the dinner party

Scholarship is a dinner party that started long before you walked in. People have been arguing about your topic for years. You don't burst through the door and shout your opinion over the appetizers.

You find a seat. You listen. You figure out who's saying what, and who disagrees. Then you join in a way that shows you were paying attention: "Smith and Jones have been making the case for X; Lee isn't convinced; here's what I'd add." ( called this the "unending conversation" in a parlor.)

Your literature review is you earning your seat at the table, before you say your own piece.

Step 2: the three moves to join in

1. Report what they say, fairly

Don't drop a quote and run. Frame it: who is talking, and what are they doing? A plus a precise verb does the work.

Verbs that carry meaning: argues, finds, shows, reports, observes, suggests, contends, warns, cautions, concludes. ("Says" is fine in moderation, but the others tell your reader more.)

Smith (2021) argues that shoppers routinely misread "natural."

2. Weave, don't list

The difference between a summary and a is putting voices in the same breath. Group agreement, name tension.

Several studies (Smith 2021; Jones 2020) find that vague terms mislead shoppers, though Lee (2022) cautions that some see right through them.

3. Add your voice, turn to the gap

Once the conversation is on the page, step in. This is where your review points at your own study.

What this conversation has not addressed is ___, which is where my study enters.

All three moves, one paragraph

"Research on food labeling consistently finds that shoppers misread vague terms: Smith (2021) shows that most read 'natural' as 'organic,' and Jones (2020) reports the same confusion around 'farm fresh.' This is less a failure of attention than of design. Regulators leave 'natural' almost undefined (FDA, 2022), and industry groups have fought to keep it that way (Lee, 2023). What this conversation has not asked is what that engineered confusion costs the small, honest producer, the very question this study takes up."

Notice: report (Smith shows, Jones reports), weave (two voices in one sentence, then a second pair), turn to the gap (what nobody asked). You never wrote "Source 1 says."

✍ On your own

Read your paragraph aloud when you're done. Does it sound like you joined a conversation, or like a list of people talking past each other? Fix the spot where it turns back into a list.

Signal phrase

A few words that name who is talking and frame their point before you quote or paraphrase: "Smith argues...," "According to Jones...," "As the FDA notes...." It hands your reader the source's identity and your take on it in one move, so a quote never lands out of nowhere.

Synthesis

Combining several sources into a single point (what they reveal together) instead of summarizing them one at a time. "Three studies agree X, but one complicates it" is synthesis. "Smith says... Jones says... Lee says..." is a list. Synthesis is the whole job of a literature review.

Who is Kenneth Burke?

An American literary theorist (1897–1993). His famous "parlor" image: you show up at a party where a heated conversation is already going. Nobody stops to catch you up. You listen a while, get the drift, and then put in your oar. Scholarship works exactly like that, and so does your literature review.
