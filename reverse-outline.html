Reverse Outline

The best way to see your draft's real structure is to outline what you actually wrote, not what you meant to. One line per paragraph. The skeleton it reveals shows the gaps, repetition, and out-of-order pieces the prose hides.

This is , not editing. Editing fixes sentences; revision re-sees the whole. Go through your draft paragraph by paragraph and write, in a few words, the job that paragraph does (not what it's about, what it does: "introduces the gap," "reports the survey numbers," "interprets the trust finding"). Tag each to its section. Then read the skeleton.

2 · Your report's skeleton

    Use it to revise, in this order: fix the first, a missing section, paragraphs out of order, two paragraphs doing the same job, a section that's thin. Sentence and comma fixes come after. Rearranging a skeleton is a lot easier than rewriting finished prose, which is exactly why you outline backward.

    Revision vs. editing

    They're different jobs. Revision (literally "re-seeing") works at the level of ideas and structure: Is the argument clear? Is anything missing, repeated, or out of order? Editing works at the sentence level, and proofreading catches typos. Do them in that order, big to small. Most weak drafts were edited but never truly revised.

    IMRAD

    Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion, the standard shape of a research report. Tagging each paragraph to its section is how you check that every section exists, that they run in order, and that nothing has wandered into the wrong room (interpretation stuck in Results, a finding buried in the Introduction).

    Higher-order concerns first

    Writing centers sort revision into higher-order concerns (focus, organization, development, whether the argument holds) and lower-order concerns (grammar, citations, formatting). Fix the higher-order ones first, there's no point polishing the commas in a paragraph you're about to cut. The reverse outline is a higher-order tool by design.
