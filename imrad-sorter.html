Build the Report

A research report has a shared shape so any reader can navigate it fast. It's called . Below are ten pieces of a food-label study, scrambled. Drop each one into the section where it belongs.

The four sections each answer one question. : what's my question, and why does it matter? : how did I study it? : what did I find? : so what, and now what?
Introduction ← your P1 lit review
Methods + Results ← your P2 & P3
Discussion ← new writing that ties it together
Good news: you've already written most of it

On your own: pick a section for each piece, then open the answer. Notice which project each piece came from, you're seeing your whole semester become one report.

Piece 1

"Prior studies disagree on whether 'natural' labels shape shopper trust, and almost none look at small-town or student shoppers."

Where does it go?
Introductionfrom your P1 This is your literature review setting up the gap, what's known, what's missing, why your study is needed. That's the job of the Introduction.

Piece 2

"This study asks: do sustainability labels actually inform shoppers, and who gets misled when they don't?"

Where does it go?
Introductionyour research question Your central question and its significance close out the Introduction. It tells the reader exactly what the rest of the report will answer.

Piece 3

"I coded fifteen product packages for claim type and specificity, then surveyed forty-two adult shoppers with an anonymous Google Form."

Where does it go?
Methodsfrom your P2 & P3 Exactly how you studied it, both the qualitative coding and the quantitative survey, goes in Methods. Enough detail that someone could repeat your study.

Piece 4

"Participants were recruited through three online groups and in person at a campus dining hall; no identifying information was collected."

Where does it go?
Methodsyour recruitment plan How you recruited and how you protected participants is part of Methods too. It shows your study was systematic and ethical.

Piece 5

"Sixty-two percent of respondents said they look for an organic seal 'often' or 'always' (Figure 1)."

Where does it go?
Resultsfrom your P3 A number plus its figure, reported plainly with no interpretation yet. Results states what you found. What it means waits for the Discussion.

Piece 6

"Only 31% said they trust the word 'natural,' the lowest of any label tested. In open responses, several called it meaningless; one wrote, 'It's on everything.'"

Where does it go?
ResultsP3 numbers + quotes Participant quotes are data. Presenting them next to the matching statistic still belongs in Results, that's your mixed-methods evidence. You interpret it in the Discussion.

Piece 7

"These results suggest shoppers are far more skeptical of vague claims like 'natural' than of certified ones like 'organic.'"

Where does it go?
Discussionyour interpretation The word "suggest" is the tell. Here you make sense of the numbers and connect them back to your question. Interpretation lives in the Discussion, not the Results.

Piece 8

"I recommend that stores mark which claims are third-party certified, so shoppers can tell a real standard from a marketing word at a glance."

Where does it go?
Discussionyour recommendation The "now what," a concrete, actionable recommendation, is where a good Discussion ends. This is also where your social-justice lens pays off: what should change, and for whom?

Piece 9

"Because the sample was small and skewed young, these findings are suggestive rather than conclusive and can't speak for all shoppers."

Where does it go?
Discussionyour limitations Limitations usually sit near the end of the Discussion (some writers put them in Methods, either is defensible). Naming them makes you more credible, not less.

Piece 10

"Roughly seventy percent of new grocery products now carry some kind of 'eco' claim, yet shoppers report growing confusion about what those claims mean."

Where does it go?
Introductionyour opening context The broad, real-world hook that opens the report and shows why the topic matters. Introductions start wide like this, then narrow to your specific question (Piece 2).

IMRAD

The standard structure of a research report: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Nearly every scientific and social-science paper uses it, which means readers know exactly where to look for each thing. Learning IMRAD once lets you read, and write, research in any field.

Introduction

Answers "what's my question, and why should anyone care?" It opens broad (real-world context), reviews what's already known (your literature review), names the gap, and lands on your specific research question. Most of this is your P1 work, revised to flow.

Methods

Answers "how did I study it?" You describe your instrument, your participants and how you recruited them, and how you analyzed the data, both the qualitative and quantitative sides. The test: could someone repeat your study from this description? Written in past tense, plainly.

Results

Answers "what did I find?" Just the findings: your numbers, percentages, captioned figures, and participant quotes, reported without interpretation. Resist explaining what it all means here; that's the next section. Results is the evidence; Discussion is the argument.

Discussion

Answers "so what, and now what?" You interpret your results, connect them back to your question and your sources, admit your limitations, and, crucially for this course, make a concrete recommendation. This is the most original writing in the report, and often the most important.

Abstract

A short paragraph (roughly 150 to 250 words) summarizing the whole report: question, method, key finding, and takeaway. It sits near the top but you write it last, once you know what the report actually says. Think of it as the movie trailer for your study.

Figure captions

Every chart or table gets a number and a caption ("Figure 1. How often shoppers look for an organic seal, N = 42"). Captions let a reader understand the visual without hunting through your text, and they make your report look, and read, like real research. Number them in the order they appear.

Appendix

The back-of-report home for material a reader might want but that would clog the main text: a copy of your full survey or interview guide, and your raw or summarized data. Putting your instrument in the appendix is what lets someone judge, or repeat, your study. Label them "Appendix A," "Appendix B," and refer to them from the Methods.
