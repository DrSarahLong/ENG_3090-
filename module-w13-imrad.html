IMRAD & Assembling Your Report

The final report isn't a new writing project. It's the assembly of everything you've already made, into one shape readers know how to navigate. Budget about an hour, then start assembling.

Read this first, it changes the whole week. You are not starting a report from a blank page. You have a literature review (P1), a qualitative analysis (P2), and a quantitative analysis (P3) already written. This week you pour them into a standard container and write the connective tissue. As the , your job now is to make three strong pieces read as one whole study.

Step 1: what IMRAD is, and why every field uses it

stands for Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. It's the shape of nearly every research report across the sciences and social sciences. The point is shared navigation: a reader who knows IMRAD can find your method or your findings in seconds, without hunting. Learn it once and you can read, and write, real research in any field.

Step 2: the four sections, as four questions

  • what's my question, and why does it matter?
  • how did I study it?
  • what did I find?
  • so what, and now what?

Keep Results and Discussion distinct. Results reports the findings with no spin. Discussion is where you interpret them. Mixing the two is the most common way student reports lose a reader's trust.

Step 3: how your three projects slot in

  • P1 (Literature Review) becomes most of your Introduction, revised so it flows into your question.
  • P2 (Qualitative) and P3 (Quantitative) supply your Methods and Results, both approaches, side by side.
  • The Discussion is your newest writing: what it all means, your recommendation, your limits.

So the actual new drafting this week is smaller than it looks: a strong Discussion, an Abstract, transitions between sections, and a design pass. The heavy lifting is already done.

Step 4: the hourglass shape

A good report is shaped like an . The Introduction opens broad (the real-world issue) and narrows to your exact question. Methods and Results are the narrow, specific middle. The Discussion widens back out, from your findings to what they mean for the world.

Step 5: the parts around IMRAD

  • Title page: a clear, specific title, your name, the course, the date.
  • : a short summary of the whole thing. Write it last.
  • References: your full source list in APA. By the final report this should reach 15 to 20 sources, and they don't all need to be scholarly.
  • : your instrument (the actual survey or interview guide) and your data. This is what lets a reader judge or repeat your work.

Step 6: document design is access

This is a technical writing course, so how the report looks is part of the grade, and part of the ethics. Good decides who can actually use your report. A wall of gray text excludes tired, rushed, or struggling readers. Clear design includes them.

  • Headings that match IMRAD, so readers can scan and jump.
  • Captioned ("Figure 1. …"), numbered in order and referenced in the text.
  • Consistent formatting: one font scheme, even spacing, aligned headings, page numbers.
  • White space. Room to breathe is not wasted space; it's what makes a page readable.

Step 7: make it one story, not three stapled projects

The seams will show if you just paste P1, P2, and P3 together. Smooth them with and one consistent voice. The report should read as a single arc: here's the gap I found, here's how I studied it, here's what I found, and here's what we should do about it. Read it start to finish out loud, and every place you stumble is a seam to fix.

✍ On your own

Say your whole study in one sentence: "Because [gap], I [method], and found [finding], so [recommendation]." If you can say that cleanly, your report has a spine. If you can't yet, that's the thing to figure out before you assemble.

Primary vs. secondary research

Secondary research analyzes sources others made (your literature review). Primary research generates new data (your survey and analysis). Your final report is the full arc of a primary study: you used secondary work to find the gap, then primary work to fill it. Assembling it is the last step of becoming the investigator, not just the reader.

IMRAD

The standard structure of a research report: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Nearly every scientific and social-science paper uses it, so readers always know where to look. Learn it once and you can navigate research in any field.

Introduction

Answers "what's my question, and why care?" Opens broad with real-world context, reviews what's known (your literature review), names the gap, and lands on your specific question. Mostly your P1, revised to flow toward that question.

Methods

Answers "how did I study it?" Your instrument, your participants and recruiting, and how you analyzed both the qualitative and quantitative data. The test: could someone repeat your study from this? Past tense, plain and complete.

Results

Answers "what did I find?" The findings only: numbers, percentages, captioned figures, participant quotes, with no interpretation. Save the meaning for the Discussion. Results is the evidence; Discussion is the argument built on it.

Discussion

Answers "so what, and now what?" Interpret your results, tie them back to your question and sources, admit limitations, and make a concrete recommendation. This is your most original writing, and where your social-justice lens turns findings into a call to change something.

The hourglass shape

A way to picture a report's flow. Wide at the top (broad context), narrowing through your specific question, into the tight middle of Methods and Results, then widening again in the Discussion out to bigger implications. Start general, get specific, end general. It keeps readers oriented the whole way down.

Abstract

A short paragraph (roughly 150 to 250 words) summarizing question, method, key finding, and takeaway. It sits near the top, but write it last, once you know what your report actually says. It's the trailer for your study, the thing many people read instead of the whole paper.

Appendix

The back-of-report home for material a reader might want but that would clog the main text: your full survey or interview guide, and your raw or summarized data. Including your instrument is what lets someone judge or repeat your study. Label them "Appendix A," "Appendix B," and point to them from the Methods.

Figure captions

Every chart or table gets a number and a caption ("Figure 1. How often shoppers look for an organic seal, N = 42"). A caption lets readers understand the visual without digging through your prose, and it makes the report read like real research. Number figures in the order they appear and mention each one in the text.

Document design

How a document looks and is organized on the page, headings, spacing, type, figures, alignment. In technical writing this isn't decoration; it's access. Design decides who can actually read and use your work. A clear, well-structured page welcomes readers; a dense, inconsistent one quietly turns them away.

Signposting & transitions

The small cues that guide a reader between ideas and sections ("Having established the gap, I turn to how I studied it…"). They're what make separate pieces feel like one argument. Without them, an assembled report reads as three documents in a trench coat. A sentence of transition at each section seam does most of the work.
