1 · The shape of the report

A discourse-analysis paper has four moving parts. You met them in the lit review; here they wrap around your own analysis instead of someone else's.

  1. Introduction. Your question, why it matters, and a sentence or two of context (what others have said about texts like yours).
  2. . What you actually did: your (how many texts, what kind, from where), your , and any tools.
  3. . The patterns you found, each with a number and a quote. This is the evidence.
  4. . What the patterns mean, whose interest they serve, who's missing, and what your analysis can't claim ().
See it — the four parts in our example

Take our running egg-carton example. The introduction asks whether the sustainability words on cartons tell shoppers the truth. Methods says "eighteen cartons from two Boone groceries, coded for claim type." Findings report the counts and the exact wording. Discussion says who those words serve and who they mislead.

That's our example, not your topic. Your paper runs the same four parts on the texts you gathered.

3 · A finding needs a number AND a quote

This is where papers live or die. A finding isn't "the labels felt manipulative." A finding is the pattern, how often it appeared, and the exact words that show it. Don't paraphrase your evidence; use the real words.

See it — number plus quote

From our egg-carton example: "Eleven of eighteen cartons used an appeal to nature, including one that read 'gathered the way nature intended.'" The count tells the reader how common the pattern is. The quote lets them hear it for themselves. Every finding in your paper carries both.

4 · The discussion is where you think

Anyone can report counts. Your discussion says what they mean: why the pattern is there, whose interest it serves, who the language leaves out. This is your justice reading, and it's the heart of the grade. Then be honest about your so a reader trusts you.

See it — the justice reading, then the limits

What it means (our example): the "natural" appeal serves the brands writing it and the shoppers who can already decode a label; it misleads shoppers who pay more for a word that means little, and it prices out small farms that can't afford certification.

Its limits (our example): eighteen cartons, two stores, one week, and it studies the labels rather than the shoppers who read them. Naming that doesn't weaken the paper. It shows you know what you found and what you'd need a bigger study to prove.

Methods

The section that tells a reader exactly what you did, clearly enough that they could repeat it: what your corpus was (how many texts, what kind, gathered from where), how you coded it (your codebook), and any tools you used.

Example: "I analyzed eighteen egg cartons photographed at two Boone groceries over one week, coding each sustainability claim as vague, certified, or unverifiable." Methods is about transparency, not persuasion. Say what you did, plainly.

Corpus

The full set of texts you gathered and analyzed, your evidence pile. It can be small, but a reader needs to know its size and shape: how many texts, what kind, and where they came from.

Example: "eighteen egg cartons from two Boone groceries" is a corpus. So is "one landlord's lease plus the city's tenant-rights page." Name yours exactly; a vague corpus makes every finding vague.

Codebook

Your list of codes and what each one means, the rulebook you used to sort the language in your corpus. Built in Entry 6a, it's what makes your counting consistent instead of a gut call that shifts as you go.

Example: a code named "appeal to nature" defined as "any claim that treats natural or wild as automatically better or safer," so you tag it the same way on carton one and carton eighteen.

Findings

What your analysis actually turned up, reported as evidence: the patterns, how often each appeared (the count), and exact quotes that show them. Findings are just the facts of your corpus. You save the meaning for the discussion.

Example: "Eleven of eighteen cartons used an appeal to nature, including one reading 'gathered the way nature intended.'" A finding without a number or without a quote is only a hunch.

Discussion

The section where your findings become meaning. It answers "so what?": why the pattern exists, whose interest it serves, what it does to a reader, and how it connects to the bigger conversation.

Example: moving from "eleven of eighteen cartons appealed to nature" to "that word reassures shoppers who can't verify it and lets brands charge more for a term that means little." This is the thinking the tools and counts can't do for you, and it's where the real credit lives.

Limitations

An honest statement of what your analysis can't claim: a small corpus, one region or one store, labels rather than the shoppers who read them.

Example: "With eighteen cartons from two Boone stores, I can describe how these labels talk, but not how shoppers across the country actually read them." Naming your limits strengthens the paper. It shows you know the difference between what you found and what you'd need a bigger study to prove.
