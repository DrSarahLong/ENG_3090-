Is This Ethical?

You're about to study real people. Six situations a lot like your own project. For each, decide: is it fine, does it need fixing first, or is it a hard no?

The through-line: before you collect a single response, ask three questions. Do they know what they're joining, and did they say yes? Could this hurt them, and how do I keep it from doing so? Am I putting the burden on people who can least push back? Answer those honestly and you're most of the way to ethical research.

The Belmont Report

A 1979 U.S. report, written after abuses like the Tuskegee syphilis study, that set the three principles all human-subjects research now follows: respect for persons (informed, voluntary participation), beneficence (maximize good, minimize harm), and justice (fairly share the risks and benefits, don't exploit the vulnerable). Your CITI training is built on it.

Informed consent

Before anyone takes part, they get a plain-language explanation of what the study is, what you'll ask, how their data will be used and protected, and that they can stop anytime, and then they freely agree. "Informed" and "voluntary" are the whole idea. No consent, no data.

Anonymous vs. confidential

These are not the same. Anonymous means you never collect anything that could identify a person, not even you can link a response to a name. Confidential means you might know who said what, but you protect it and never reveal it. If your form asks for a name or email, you can only promise confidentiality, not anonymity.

IRB (Institutional Review Board)

A committee at a university that reviews research with human participants before it begins, to keep it ethical. The key thing for you: student projects for a class at this level are exempt, so you will not go through IRB review. That's also why some topics are off the table: you can't study people in jails, schoolchildren, or other protected groups, because those need a full review a class project can't do. A short anonymous survey of adults is exactly the kind of thing that's fine.

Vulnerable populations

Groups who may not be able to freely consent or who could be harmed more easily, and so get extra protection: children, prisoners, people with cognitive impairments, and people under pressure from their situation (economic, legal, medical). Farmworkers worried about their jobs and people in recovery can both qualify. Extra care, or a different topic.
