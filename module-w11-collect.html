Recruiting & Collecting Your Data

Your instrument is built. Now you take it into the world and gather real responses. This is the most hands-on week of the semester, you're fully in the field. Budget about an hour to set up, then the collecting runs on its own.

You're in the field now. Designing the survey was the last desk step. From here on you're a doing what primary investigators do: reaching real people, gathering data no one else has, and living with whatever it tells you. Nobody can hand you these results. You go get them.

Step 1: sample vs. population

Your is everyone your question is about, all grocery shoppers, say. You obviously can't ask all of them, so you ask a sample, a smaller slice. Everything rides on whether that slice fairly stands in for the whole. Ask the wrong slice and your findings quietly shrink to "what my friends think," even though your report says "what shoppers think."

Step 2: choose who you'll ask, on purpose

  • . Whoever's easy to reach. It's what most class projects use, and it's allowed, as long as you name its limit: easy-to-reach people tend to look like you.
  • . If you recruit in person, ask every third person who passes, not the ones who "look right." That one rule strips out your own bias about who to approach.
  • . Ask each participant to pass your link to someone unlike them. A cheap way to reach past your own circle.
  • Reach a few different groups on purpose. Deliberately recruit from two or three unlike places (a budget-grocery group, a farm group, a general campus list). This is the single best move a class project can make toward a fair sample.

Here's where our lens does real work. Before you hit send, ask: whose voices am I missing? If your sample is all students, all one age, all people who already care about food labels, you're answering a smaller question than you think. isn't just fairer, it makes your findings mean more.

Step 3: how many is enough?

There's no magic number, but the honest guidance: for a survey, aim for a few dozen responses, more if you can get them, because too few and you can't tell a real pattern from random noise. For interviews, five to eight good conversations is often plenty, since depth is the point, not count. You'll likely end up with a small sample, and that's fine for a class study. What matters is that you and don't overclaim.

Step 4: get the word out (with consent attached)

Push your instrument out through more than one channel so you don't just catch one type of person:

  • A link or shared in a few different online groups.
  • In person, at a spot where varied people pass through, using your every-nth rule.
  • Email or text to a list, then a single friendly reminder.

Whatever the channel, your from Week 9 rides along every time. People should always know what they're joining before they answer.

Step 5: collect clean

  • Keep it anonymous if that's what you promised. Don't quietly add a name field mid-stream.
  • Don't coach. If someone asks what an answer "should" be, tell them there's no right answer. The moment you nudge, you've contaminated the data.
  • Don't fudge. You will be tempted to drop responses you don't like. Don't. Bad-for-your-hypothesis is not the same as invalid.
  • Watch for junk. Bots and joke responses happen with public links. It's okay to remove obvious garbage (gibberish, impossible answers), just write down what you removed and why.
  • Back it up. Download your responses (export the sheet) more than once as they come in. Losing your only copy the night before P3 is a real and avoidable tragedy.

Step 6: know when to stop

Stop when you've hit a reasonable number and fresh responses stop changing the picture. For interviews, that's , when new conversations mostly echo ones you've already had. Give it the time it needs (a few days to a week of live collecting), then close it so you have room to analyze next week.

✍ On your own

Look at where you're recruiting and picture the people who'll never see your survey there. Who are they? Name one group you're missing and one concrete way to reach them, then actually do it before you close collection.

Now: launch and log it (Notebook Entry 11b)

Record your recruitment plan going live, keep a short running log of responses and any problems, and note when you'll stop. Do not analyze yet, that's next week. This week you just gather clean data.

Primary vs. secondary research

Secondary research means working with what already exists: reading, evaluating, and analyzing sources other people produced. Your literature review and your analysis of food labels were both secondary. Primary research means generating new data yourself, by surveying, interviewing, or observing real people. Collecting responses this week is primary research in its purest form, you're the one producing the knowledge.

Sample vs. population

Your population is everyone your question is really about (say, "grocery shoppers"). You can't ask all of them, so you ask a sample, a smaller slice. The whole game is choosing a slice that fairly stands in for the whole. A skewed sample answers a narrower question than the one you meant to ask.

Convenience sampling

Recruiting whoever is easiest to reach, your friends, your class, people walking by. It's what most class projects use, and that's okay, as long as you name its limit: the people who are easy for you to reach tend to resemble you, which quietly narrows your findings. Counter it by reaching into a few groups outside your usual circle.

A simple sampling rule

When you recruit in person, decide a rule in advance, ask every third person who walks by, for example, and stick to it. It sounds trivial, but it takes the choice of "who looks approachable" out of your hands, and that choice is where a lot of bias sneaks in. A rule recruits more fairly than your gut does.

Snowball sampling

You ask each participant to pass your survey along to others. It's an easy way to grow a sample and reach people you couldn't find yourself. The catch: friends refer friends, so the snowball tends to stay similar. Nudge it wider by asking people to share with someone unlike them.

Representative sample

A sample that mirrors the larger group you care about, so what you find in the sample is a fair guess about the population. Big professional studies use random sampling to get there. Class projects rarely can, so you aim for reasonable spread instead: deliberately reach several different kinds of people, and be honest about who you did and didn't reach.

Naming your limitations

Every study has them, and saying so out loud makes you more credible, not less. A small or convenience sample is a limitation, so in your report you write a sentence like, "This sample of 34 mostly-student shoppers can't represent all consumers, so these findings are suggestive, not conclusive." Honest limits are the mark of a real researcher.

QR code

A scannable square that opens your survey link when someone points a phone camera at it. Google Forms and Qualtrics both give you a shareable link you can turn into a free QR code (search "free QR code generator"). Handy for in-person recruiting, print it on a half-sheet and let people scan and take the survey on their own phone.

Informed consent

Before anyone takes part, they get a plain-language explanation of what the study is, what you'll ask, how their data will be used and protected, and that they can stop anytime, and then they freely agree. You wrote this in Week 9. It travels with your survey through every channel, no consent, no data.

Saturation

The point in interview research where new conversations mostly repeat what you've already heard, so more interviews stop teaching you much. It's a common, defensible reason to stop collecting qualitative data. For surveys the logic is looser, but the same idea applies: when fresh responses stop shifting the overall picture, you've likely got enough.
