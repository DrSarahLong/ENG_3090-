ENG 3090 · Week 6, Tuesday

Code These Labels

Last week you noticed patterns. Now you name them. This is : tagging each text by the move it's making, so the pattern becomes countable.

On your own, in two moves: first name your own (Step 1), then use a shared to tag six taglines and see which move runs the show (Step 2).

N · Appeal to nature V · Vague virtue words D · Manufactured distrust E · Emotion / family

Step 1 · Build the codes yourself

These taglines keep making the same handful of moves. Before you borrow anyone's labels, name your own. Read these four and jot 2 to 4 categories you'd use:

  • "Gathered the way nature intended."
  • "Real, wholesome goodness in every bite."
  • "No funny business, just honest eggs."
  • "The eggs your family deserves."

A model codebook (one good version)

CodeDefinitionExample
N · Appeal to natureClaims that "natural" or "farm fresh" means better or safer"the way nature intended"
V · Vague virtue wordsWarm words you can't measure or check"real, wholesome goodness"
D · Manufactured distrustImplies other brands are dishonest, without naming one"no funny business"
E · Emotion / familyMoves feelings about loved ones onto the product"the eggs your family deserves"

Yours won't match word-for-word, and that's fine, there's no single right codebook. What matters is that each code is clear enough to apply the same way every time.

Step 2 · Now code with this codebook

"Gathered the way nature intended."
N · Appeal to nature. "The way nature intended" tells you natural equals better, with no evidence.
"Real, wholesome goodness in every bite."
V · Vague virtue words. "Real," "wholesome," "goodness": warm words you can't measure or check.
"No funny business, just honest eggs."
D · Manufactured distrust. Implies the other brands are up to funny business, without naming one.
"The eggs your family deserves."
E · Emotion / family. Says nothing about the eggs; moves your feelings about your family onto them.
"100% all-natural, farm fresh."
N · Appeal to nature. "All-natural" and "farm fresh" are the nature move again, and both are unregulated.
"Nothing to hide, unlike the other guys."
D · Manufactured distrust. Even more direct: it names "the other guys" as the ones with something to hide.

What the counts show

N · Appeal to nature 2
D · Manufactured distrust 2
V · Vague virtue words 1
E · Emotion / family 1

Now the move that separates coding from just : so what? Two moves dominate, selling "nature" and stoking suspicion of everyone else. Neither is a claim you could check. The pattern isn't random; it's a strategy, and it works on the shopper with the least time to think. That sentence, backed by your counts, is the heart of your analysis.

Coding

Tagging each text by the move it makes (an appeal, a framing, a recurring word) so patterns become countable instead of just a vibe. It turns "these labels feel manipulative" into "11 of 15 use an appeal to nature." (Johnny Saldaña's Coding Manual for Qualitative Researchers is the standard guide.)

A code

One named category in your analysis, like "appeal to nature." A good code has a short definition and a clear example, so you (and anyone else) apply it the same way every time. Codes are the buckets you sort your texts into.

Codebook

Your full list of codes, each with its definition and an example. It's what makes your analysis systematic rather than just your impression: hand your codebook to a classmate and they should be able to tag the same texts and land close to your results. (That's called intercoder reliability.)

Content analysis

The counting cousin of discourse analysis: how often each code shows up across your corpus. Discourse analysis asks "how is this working?"; content analysis asks "how often, and where?" Strong projects use both. (Klaus Krippendorff's Content Analysis is the standard handbook.)
