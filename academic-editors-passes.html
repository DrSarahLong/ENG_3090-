Signal phrase

A few words that name whose voice you are quoting and frame their point before the quotation lands: "Smith argues that…," "According to the FDA…," "As Nestle notes…." It hands the reader the source's identity and your take in one move, so a quote never appears out of nowhere.

The quote sandwich

Three layers: a signal phrase to introduce it, the quote itself, and then your own sentence saying what it means and why it matters for your argument. A quote dropped in with no top and no bottom slice makes the reader do your interpreting for you. Example bottom slice: "That phrasing matters because it lets a label imply a health benefit the product never earned."

Hedging

Language that matches a claim to the strength of its evidence: "suggests," "is associated with," "in this sample," "tends to." The opposite is overclaiming ("proves," "always," "everyone"). Data from one small study rarely proves anything. WITHOUT hedge: "Organic labels always fool shoppers." WITH hedge: "In this sample, the word 'organic' was more likely to be read as a health claim." The hedged version is the one a careful reader trusts.

Peer-reviewed

Vetted by other experts in the field before publication. It is not a guarantee of truth, but it is a real filter. An industry marketing blog, a press release, or a company's own "research" page has not been through that filter, so it needs more scrutiny before you lean on it, especially when the source profits from the claim.

Signposting

The topic sentences and transitions that tell a reader where they are and where they are headed. Read only the first sentence of each paragraph in a row: if that chain still tells the story of your argument, your signposting is doing its job. If the chain reads as disconnected facts, the reader is doing the connecting you should have done.
