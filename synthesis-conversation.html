The Sources Are Talking

Four findings from real research on food labels. They're already in conversation. Your job: hear it.

On your own: sort each snippet into one of two . Then hit "Reveal the conversation" to see how they weave together, and where the is.

Theme A: what shoppers understand Theme B: who controls the words
"A 2021 study found most shoppers believed 'natural' meant 'organic,' though the two are legally unrelated."
Snippet 1
"Regulators define 'organic' tightly but leave 'natural' almost entirely unregulated, so marketers can use it freely."
Snippet 2

Here's the conversation these four are having

Theme A (what shoppers understand): Snippets 1 and 3 agree that shoppers routinely misread vague terms, and that the feeling a label gives ("healthier," "organic") runs ahead of any real meaning.

Theme B (who controls the words): Snippets 2 and 4 agree that this vagueness isn't an accident, it's protected. Regulators leave the terms loose, and industry fights to keep them that way.

Put together, the conversation says: shoppers are confused (A) because the words are deliberately left fuzzy by the people who profit from them (B). That's , not a list. Notice you never wrote "Snippet 1 says..."; you wrote what the sources, together, reveal.

And the gap

All four study the shopper or the regulator. None of them ask what the confusion does to the honest small producer, the one doing real regenerative work who can't out-shout the greenwashing. None ask the shoppers with the least time or money to decode a label. That silence is the opening for a study. It's also a justice gap: a missing voice.

Synthesis

Synthesis means putting sources into conversation and saying what they reveal together, as one combined point. It's the opposite of a list that summarizes source 1, then source 2, then source 3.

The tell: you're writing about the idea and the sources back you up, instead of marching through them one at a time.

Theme

A theme is a shared idea that several sources have in common, the bucket you can group them into. Finding themes is how a pile of sources turns into a structure.

Here, two snippets share "what shoppers understand" and two share "who controls the words."

The gap

The gap is the question the existing research hasn't answered yet: the missing piece, the voice nobody asked. Naming it is how you justify a new study, because your work fills the gap.

In this course the gap is often a justice question: who got left out of the conversation?
