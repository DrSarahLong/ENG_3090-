3 · What the numbers say

    Before this goes in your report: does the chart start at a fair baseline, is every axis and slice labeled, and did you state your ? A reader should get the true story at a glance, not the story you wish were true. That's the whole difference between analysis and spin.

    Frequency table

    A simple count of how many people chose each answer. If 14 of your 42 respondents picked "Sometimes," that's a frequency. Building one is the first step of any quantitative analysis: it turns a column of individual responses into countable totals you can compare and chart. In Google Sheets, COUNTIF tallies them for you.

    Descriptive statistics

    Plain numbers that summarize your data, no advanced math required. The ones you'll actually use: the count (how many, your N), the percentage for each answer, and the mode (the most common answer). For genuinely numeric data you can add a mean (average) or median (middle value). You do not need regression or p-values for this project.

    Match the chart to the data

    Bar chart: comparing separate categories or answer choices (the workhorse, almost always safe). Pie chart: parts of one whole that add to 100%, and only when you have a few slices. Line chart: change over time. Stacked or grouped bars: comparing groups against each other. When in doubt, a clean bar chart is rarely wrong.

    The truncated-axis trick

    The most common way charts lie: instead of starting the vertical axis at zero, you start it near the data so small differences look enormous. A jump from 48% to 52% can be drawn to look like a landslide. Flip the toggle to see it happen to your own data. Then never do it, start bars at zero, and you can't accidentally deceive anyone.

    N (your sample size)

    N is how many responses your chart is built on. Always report it ("N = 42"), because a percentage means something very different at N = 8 than at N = 800. A small N isn't shameful, it's normal for a class project, but hiding it lets a reader assume more certainty than your data can support. State it, own it.
