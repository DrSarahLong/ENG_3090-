Run Your Corpus Through the Tools

Let the machine count what would take you hours, so you can spend your time on the meaning. First: your corpus has to be saved as searchable text. Budget about 45 minutes.

Step 1: Voyant (start here, nothing to install)

Voyant Tools is free and runs in your browser. Paste your whole corpus into the box and hit Reveal. In seconds you get:

  • A word cloud and a list, your most common words, which are your patterns in numbers.
  • Contexts (a ), click any word to see every place it appears with the words around it.
  • , the words that keep showing up near your key words.

Step 2: going deeper (optional)

AntConc is a free download that does heavier concordance and collocation work, and can measure which words are unusually common in your corpus. laurenceanthony.net

QDA Miner is the industry-standard software for hands-on qualitative coding, and App State has a free campus license to the full version (ask Belk Library or your department for access). Fair warning: it takes real training to use well, more than an afternoon. But if you're eyeing grad school, or any job that touches research, it's worth the time. Specialized software comes up in interviews, and being able to say you have genuine familiarity with QDA Miner, even short of mastery, is a real edge. provalisresearch.com

Google Sheets is all you need to turn your code counts into a clean bar chart for your paper.

Step 3: AI as an assistant (not the analyst)

AI is genuinely useful here: paste your corpus and ask it to suggest themes, group similar codes, or summarize what stands out. It's fast and it sees things.

⚠ Keep the wheel

AI will also confidently make things up, and it will hand you a tidy reading that flattens what's actually interesting. So: use it to suggest, then check every suggestion against your real texts. You own the analysis. If AI proposes a code, you don't keep it until you've found the examples yourself. The thinking is the assignment.

Step 4: fold it into your analysis

The tools gave you numbers and patterns. Now connect them back to your codes: which counts back up a code you named? Did the tool surface a pattern you missed? Did anything contradict your first read (that's gold, not a problem)?

✍ On your own

Find one thing the tool showed you that you would not have seen by hand. Write it in a sentence. That's the payoff of the whole week.

Word frequency

A simple count of how often each word appears in your corpus, usually after dropping filler words like "the" and "of." It's the fastest way to see what a set of texts keeps returning to, and it turns "it felt like a lot" into a number.

Concordance (KWIC)

A list of every place a word appears, shown with the words on either side, so you can read how it's actually used. KWIC means "keyword in context." Frequency tells you how often; a concordance tells you how. It's the most useful thing these tools do.

Collocation

The words that tend to appear right next to a given word, its regular company. If "natural" almost always travels with "fresh," "family," and "goodness," those collocates reveal what the word is really doing, even when it claims nothing on its own.
