Run Your Corpus Through the Tools

Let the machine count what would take you hours, so you can spend your time on the meaning. Work through this at your own pace; budget about 75 minutes.

Arrive. Open your Research Notebook in another tab and take a breath. In Week 6 you coded your corpus by hand and named your patterns. By now you have a coded corpus and a codebook. Today you let a machine count all of it in seconds, then you do the reading it can't.

1 · The move: reading at a distance

Close reading means going through a text line by line with your own eyes. That's what you did in Week 6, and it's irreplaceable. But your eyes get tired, and they miss things across fifteen or twenty texts. A text tool reads the other direction: it counts every word in the whole at once and shows you patterns from a height. Researchers call this , and it's the engine under this week.

See it

Read one egg carton by hand and you notice the word "natural." Run our whole example corpus of a dozen food-labeling news articles through a tool and you learn that "natural" appears 61 times, always near "means," "assume," and "regulated," while "worker" and "cost" barely show up at all. One is a close read. The other is a pattern you could never hold in your head. Both are real evidence.

Heads up: those food-labeling articles are our running example all semester, not your topic. Watch the move on the example, then run it on the texts you actually gathered.

Corpus Analysis with Voyant Tools — Silvia Gutiérrez De la Torre, Programming Historian, 2022. A hands-on, no-install tutorial for building a corpus and reading text at a distance, the same tool you'll use in a minute. Skim the setup, then follow along in the "reading the results" sections.

One catch: short texts like labels won't give a tool much to chew on. If your corpus is a stack of tiny labels, gather the longer texts around them too (news articles, reports, brand web pages), or lean on your hand-coding. Tools reward length.

2 · What the tool hands back

Feed a tool your searchable text and it returns three things worth knowing by name. Each one answers a different question.

  • 1
    A list and a word cloud. Your most common words, ranked. This is your patterns in raw numbers, and the cloud is the same data as a fast picture.
  • 2
    A . Click any word and see every place it appears with the words around it. Frequency tells you how often; a concordance tells you how. It's the most useful thing these tools do.
  • 3
    . The words that keep showing up near your key words, their regular company. Company reveals what a word is really doing.

3 · Run your own corpus through Voyant

Voyant Tools is free, runs in your browser, and needs nothing installed. Paste your whole corpus into the box, hit Reveal, and in seconds you get the word cloud, the frequency list, and a clickable concordance from Section 2, all at once. Start here every time.

4 · Going deeper (optional)

Voyant covers what most of you need. If you want to push further, three tools earn the time.

AntConc is a free download that does heavier concordance and collocation work, and can measure which words are unusually common in your corpus compared with ordinary language. laurenceanthony.net

QDA Miner is the industry-standard software for hands-on qualitative coding, and App State has a free campus license to the full version (ask Belk Library or your department for access). Fair warning: it takes real training to use well, more than an afternoon. But if you're eyeing grad school, or any job that touches research, it's worth the time. Specialized software comes up in interviews, and being able to say you have genuine familiarity with QDA Miner, even short of mastery, is a real edge. provalisresearch.com

Google Sheets is all you need to turn your code counts into a clean bar chart for your paper. No new software, no learning curve.

5 · AI as an assistant, not the analyst

AI is genuinely useful here. Paste your corpus and ask it to suggest themes, group similar codes, or summarize what stands out. It's fast, and it notices things you might not.

⚠ Keep the wheel. AI will also confidently make things up, and it will hand you a tidy reading that flattens what's actually interesting. So use it to suggest, then check every suggestion against your real texts. You own the analysis. If AI proposes a code, you don't keep it until you've found the examples yourself in your corpus. The thinking is the assignment.

6 · Fold it into your analysis

The tools gave you numbers and patterns. Now connect them back to the codes you named in Week 6. Three questions do the work:

  • Which counts back up a code you already named by hand?
  • Did the tool surface a pattern you missed on your first read?
  • Did anything contradict your first read? That's gold, not a problem. A surprise is where the interesting finding usually hides.

Corpus

Your set of texts to analyze, gathered on purpose, the same body of texts you built in Week 5. "Corpus" just means a "body" of texts. For a tool to find real patterns you want at least 15 items of the same kind (news articles, labels, ads, policies, posts), and longer texts work better than short ones.

Example: eighteen egg-carton labels from three stores is a corpus. So is a dozen news articles about food labeling.

Distant reading

Reading a whole body of texts by counting patterns across all of them, instead of reading one closely. Franco Moretti, a literary scholar, coined the phrase in a 2000 essay, arguing that some things about a large collection of texts only show up when you step back and measure the whole thing at once.

Example: no close read of a single article tells you that "natural" appears 61 times across the corpus while "worker" appears twice. Distant reading does, and that gap is a finding.

Word frequency

A simple count of how often each word appears in your corpus, usually after dropping filler words like "the" and "of." It's the fastest way to see what a set of texts keeps returning to, and it turns "it felt like a lot" into a number.

Example: in our food-labeling corpus, "natural" tops the list at 61, "organic" at 38, "regulators" way down at 16. The ranking is the story in miniature.

Concordance (KWIC)

A list of every place a word appears, shown with the words on either side, so you can read how it's actually used. KWIC means "keyword in context." Frequency tells you how often; a concordance tells you how. It's the most useful thing these tools do.

Example: pull "natural" and you see "the word natural has no legal meaning," "shoppers assume natural means healthier." The count never showed you that; the concordance does.

Collocation

The words that tend to appear right next to a given word, its regular company. Company reveals what a word is really doing, even when the word itself claims nothing.

Example: if "natural" almost always travels with "fresh," "family," and "goodness," those collocates are setting a wholesome mood. But in news articles about labeling, "natural" travels with "means," "assume," and "regulated," a debate about the word, not an ad.
