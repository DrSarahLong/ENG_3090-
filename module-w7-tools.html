4 · Describe your corpus · Methods, paragraph one

Read the numbers off Voyant's summary panel and write the paragraph. This goes into P2 nearly as-is.

This analysis draws on a corpus of [N] texts totaling [N] words, gathered from [what kinds of sources] published between [dates]. Documents range from [shortest] to [longest] words. Texts were selected because [your inclusion rule]; I excluded [what, and why]. The corpus is small enough that I read every text closely, and I make no claim that it represents [the larger population].

Overstating is the fastest way to lose a reader you already had. Claim your corpus's worth and a reader trusts your corpus. Claim that six articles reveal how America talks about your topic and they stop trusting the parts you got right.

5 · Check your codebook against the word cloud

Open Cirrus. You're not looking for findings here. You're checking whether your codes have anything to grab onto in these particular texts.

  • Which words would you build a code around that you hadn't thought of?
  • Which of your existing codes look like they'll find nothing in this corpus? Those either need rewriting or cutting.
  • What did you expect to dominate that turns out to be rare? That gap is worth a note.

Then close it. A word cloud shows frequency and nothing else, strips every word out of context, and makes size look like importance. Never put one in P2 as evidence.

6 · Build each code's lexical footprint

This is the step that makes your coding checkable by somebody else, which is what the rubric means by a codebook that's "precise and consistent."

From the cloud and the frequency list, write down every word that might signal your code.
Run Concordance on each one and read the actual lines. Cross out the words that turn out to mean something else.
Run Collocation on the survivors. What keeps showing up beside them? Add those.
Write the surviving list into your codebook entry as that code's footprint.
Remember Tuesday

"Safe" appeared in all three Asheville texts and meant something different every time: safe from homeless people (the merchants), safe means housed (the column), a safe place to sleep tonight (the nonprofit). The count couldn't tell them apart. The concordance could.

So a footprint is a search strategy, not an answer. It narrows your corpus to the passages worth arguing about. Arguing about them is the analysis.

7 · Apply your codes · AI takes the first pass

Two machines, two jobs. Voyant counts words and can't judge a frame. AI reads for meaning and can apply your codebook as a first pass. You verify it, resolve the hard cases, and decide what it all means.

Paste your full codebook, definitions and include/exclude rules and all, then one text. Ask it to identify every passage matching each code and to quote them exactly.
Ask it to explain why each passage qualifies. If the reason doesn't match your include rule, either your rule is vague or the model is guessing. Fixing the rule is the actual work.
Verify. For every text, check that at least two quoted passages appear word for word and do fit the code.
Build your coding table: one row per coded passage, with columns for text, the quoted passage, the code, and why it qualifies. Mark anything ambiguous as contested with one line about why.
Two failure modes. It will produce quotations that sound right and are not in your text. And it will agree with whatever frame your codebook implies, because it's built to be agreeable. Neither is a reason to avoid it. Both are reasons to check it.

Write what you did in Methods: which model, what you asked it, and how you verified. Unreported AI coding is a hole in your method that any reader can put a finger through.

8 · Count, then normalize

Raw counts favor long documents. Rates let you compare unlike things, and sometimes they dissolve a difference you thought you had.

Worked example

A 370-word opinion column has 6 safety-frame passages. A 185-word news report has 3. The column looks twice as safety-framed. Divide by length and both land at 16.2 per thousand words: the same rate. The difference you thought you found was just document length.

Your turn

For each code, record the raw count, the number of documents it appears in, and the rate per thousand words. Then check Trends: if a code is concentrated in one text, say so. "One of six texts emphasizes enforcement" is a defensible claim. "This coverage emphasizes enforcement" is not, if five texts don't.

9 · Write one finding

Four moves. Numbers, a quote, a complication, and what it means for power and justice.

Across the [N] texts, [CODE] appeared in [N] passages in [N] of [N] documents, at [N] per thousand words, concentrated in [which genres]. A representative instance: "[quote]" ([text]). The pattern is not uniform: [the text that resists it] instead [what it does]. Read together, these texts [the claim about power]. The people described in them [who speaks, who is spoken about], and that matters because [your "so what" from Step 1].

The last blank is the one that counts. It's where your literature review comes back: the gap you identified and the injustice you named. A findings paragraph that stops at the numbers is a report. One that lands on what's at stake is an analysis.

Corpus

Your set of texts to analyze, gathered on purpose. "Corpus" just means a "body" of texts. For a tool to find patterns you want texts of the same kind (news articles, policies, posts, transcripts), and longer texts work better than short ones.

Example: the three Asheville texts we loaded Tuesday were a corpus. So are fifteen news articles about your own topic.

Distant reading

Reading a whole body of texts by counting patterns across all of them, instead of reading one closely. Franco Moretti, a literary scholar, coined the phrase in a 2000 essay, arguing that some things about a large collection of texts only show up when you step back and measure the whole thing at once.

Example: no close read of one Asheville article tells you that "safe" appears in all three texts meaning three different things. Counting across the corpus does, and that gap is a finding.

Word frequency

A count of how often each word appears, usually after dropping filler words like "the" and "of." The fastest way to see what a set of texts keeps returning to, and it turns "it felt like a lot" into a number.

Example: in the Asheville corpus, "safe," "shelter," and "housing" all rank high, but the ranking alone doesn't tell you they're arguing with each other. That's what the concordance is for.

Concordance (KWIC)

A list of every place a word appears, shown with the words on either side, so you can read how it's actually used. KWIC means "keyword in context." Frequency tells you how often; a concordance tells you how. It's the most useful thing these tools do.

Example: pull "safe" from the Asheville corpus and you get "our visitors do not feel safe," "housing is what makes a person safe," and "a safe place to sleep that night." Three meanings the count could never separate.

Collocation

The words that tend to appear near a given word, its regular company. Company reveals what a word is doing, even when the word itself claims nothing.

Example: if "safe" travels with "visitors," "patrols," and "downtown" in one text and with "housing" and "door" in another, you've found two different arguments using one word.

Vocabulary density

Unique words divided by total words. Higher means more varied wording. It's a register signal, but it is very sensitive to length: a short text looks denser automatically, because it has fewer chances to repeat itself.

Example: a 185-word news report will beat a 370-word column on density for reasons that have nothing to do with style. Only compare documents of similar length, and say so in Methods.

Trends

Where a word falls across your corpus, document by document, rather than totaled up. It answers a question the total hides: is this a pattern across my texts, or one loud text?

Example: if "patrols" appears nine times and eight are in one article, you cannot write "this coverage emphasizes enforcement." You can write "one of the three texts emphasizes enforcement," which is a smaller and much more defensible claim.
