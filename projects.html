How your work is graded

Major Project Rubrics

Your semester study builds in four graded pieces. Each one is scored on a handful of criteria, and each criterion has four performance levels. Tap a project to open its rubric.

How to read a rubric. Each criterion is scored on one of four levels. Meets is the target, solid, full-credit work. Read Meets first; that's what you're aiming for on every line.
Exceeds goes beyond the target Meets the target (your goal) Developing partway there Not yet not present yet
›P1 · Literature Review15%

Your starting move: find out what's already known about your question. Track down at least ten credible sources, synthesize them (group by theme and argument, not one-by-one), and name the gap, the question the existing research hasn't answered. That gap becomes the reason your study exists. Through our lens, you also ask who the existing research centers and who it overlooks. At least 2 single-spaced pages. Submitted as a Google Doc.

Model example: Acharya & Dorpenyo, "Translation for Social Justice and Inclusivity: An Integrative Literature Review" (Technical Communication and Social Justice, 2023, open access). Notice how it groups sources by theme instead of listing them one by one, and lands on a clear gap. That's the move to copy.
›P2 · Qualitative / Discourse Analysis15%

Now you look closely at language and design themselves. Choose a set of texts tied to your topic (documents, posts, transcripts, policies, interfaces) and analyze how they make meaning, how power moves through them, who they include, who they leave out. You design the method, build a codebook, run it, and report what you found with real examples. About 3 pages. Submitted as a Google Doc.

Model example: Kirsten et al., "Off the Charts" (PLOS ONE, 2025, open access), a content analysis of science-news data visualizations. See how they build a seven-theme codebook, describe their coding step by step, and check inter-coder reliability, exactly what your Methods should show.
›P3 · Quantitative Analysis15%

Here you gather your own data and make it countable. Design an instrument (survey, interview, or focus group), recruit participants ethically (consent, privacy, anonymity), collect responses, then analyze and visualize what you find in clear charts, graphs, or tables. About 2 pages plus visuals. Submitted as a Google Doc.

Model example: Mizrachi et al., a worldwide survey of student reading preferences (PLOS ONE, 2018, open access). A clear example of reporting survey results with descriptive statistics and plenty of tables and charts, on a topic you'll relate to (print vs. screen).
›Final · IMRAD Report35%

Everything comes together. Integrate all three pieces into one professional report in IMRAD format (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion), with a title page, headings, captioned visuals, and an appendix holding your instrument and data. It should read as one coherent story, from the gap you found, to how you studied it, to what you recommend. Your reference list should reach 15 to 20 sources. Submitted as a PDF.

Model example: Bredbenner & Simon, "Video abstracts and plain language summaries…" (PLOS ONE, 2019, open access), a clean, self-contained study with unmistakable Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion sections. A good structural template. Run your own draft through the Reverse-Outline X-Ray to check your sections sit in that same IMRAD order before you submit.
