Week 1 · Thursday · your first entry

Entry 1a: What research is, and why we do it through a justice lens

Welcome to your Research Notebook, this is your first entry, and you'll add to it all semester. You might picture research as science experiments and giant surveys. In rhetoric and technical writing it usually looks different, and there are four everyday ways to answer a question. To keep it concrete, we'll follow one running example the whole course:

Do the sustainability claims on food labels, "natural," "organic," "local," "regenerative", actually tell shoppers the truth, and who gets misled?

Here's how each of the four research moves would take on that question. Read each example, then write your own one-line version in the box, the same move aimed at a topic you're starting to consider. (You lock your real topic next week, so a rough guess is fine.)

1. Read what others already found

Before you gather anything yourself, you find out what's already known. You track down articles, reports, and studies other people have written on your question. In our field this is a literature review, and it's where every real study starts.

Example (the food-label study): read existing research on eco-labels and "greenwashing", how well shoppers understand a claim like "natural" versus certified "organic."

2. Analyze the text or design itself

Sometimes the thing you study is the document, its words, its layout, its choices. You look closely at how a text makes meaning and who it includes or leaves out. In our field this is often called discourse or content analysis.

Example (the food-label study): gather real food packaging and analyze the claims, which terms are regulated, which are vague marketing, and how the design makes a product look greener than it is.

3. Ask people

Other times you go straight to people and ask. Interviews, surveys, and focus groups let you hear directly from the people a document is supposed to serve.

Example (the food-label study): ask shoppers what they think "regenerative" or "natural" means, whether they paid more for it, and whether they were right.

4. Count and measure

And sometimes you count. You turn things into numbers you can compare and chart, how often something happens, how many people, how big the gap. This is the quantitative side.

Example (the food-label study): count how many labels use unregulated buzzwords, score their reading level, and chart how many shoppers guessed a term's real meaning correctly.

A form nobody can read, a warning nobody sees, an instruction that fails the people who need it most, a group left out of the conversation. This is our , starting on day one.

You don't need perfect answers. Just start noticing where power sits in the documents and discourse around your topic. (That's the at work.)

Curiosity first, questions come later. You'll carry one of these the whole semester, so jot what genuinely interests you. For each: what text or discourse could you analyze (labels, manuals, posts, policies, transcripts)? Who could you ask?

Week 4

Entry 4a: From a pile of sources to a conversation

A literature review is not a list of summaries. It's the story of a conversation your sources are already having, and where that conversation goes quiet. That quiet spot is your gap, and your gap is the reason your study exists.

The move: group, then map the conversation

Instead of "Source 1 says... Source 2 says...", cluster your sources into 2 or 3 themes. Within each theme, notice where they agree, where they clash, and what nobody has looked at yet. (This is a simple WorkNet: mapping how your sources connect through shared terms, shared citations, and shared themes.)

Through our lens, the gap is often a missing voice: a group the research overlooked, or a question no one thought to ask. Name yours.

Discourse analysis

A close, systematic look at how real texts make meaning: their word choices, their framing, what they include and leave out, and how they move power. You're not mainly counting things (that's content analysis); you're reading like a detective to see what the language is doing. (Linguist James Paul Gee treats language as always doing something; Norman Fairclough's "critical discourse analysis" focuses on how it does power.)

Content analysis

The counting cousin of discourse analysis: how often a word, theme, or move shows up across your texts. Discourse analysis asks "how is this language working?"; content analysis asks "how often, and where?" Using both makes your project stronger: the close reading tells you what to count, and the counts prove the pattern is real. (Klaus Krippendorff's Content Analysis is the standard handbook.)

Citing unusual sources in APA

APA has clean formats for articles, but not for a cereal box. The trick is to give the same four things every citation gives: who, when, what, where.

Newspaper article: Author, A. A. (Year, Month Day). Headline of the article. Newspaper Name. https://…

Product label / packaging: Brand Name. (Year or n.d.). Product name [Egg carton label]. Retailer, City, ST.

Advertisement: Brand Name. (Year). First few words or title [Advertisement]. Platform or publication.

Social media post: Author [@handle]. (Year, Month Day). First words of the post [Post]. Platform. URL

When in doubt, describe it in [brackets] so a reader could find it. If yours is genuinely strange, ask me.

Corpus

Your set of texts to analyze, gathered on purpose. For this project, aim for at least 15 (15 to 20 is better) items of the same kind (news articles, labels, ads, policies, posts) so real patterns can show up instead of one-off quirks. "Corpus" just means a "body" of texts, and corpus linguists like Paul Baker study language exactly this way.

Codebook

Your full list of codes, each with its definition and an example. It's what makes your analysis systematic rather than just your impression: hand your codebook to a classmate and they should be able to tag the same texts and land close to your results. (That's called intercoder reliability.)

A code

One named category in your analysis, like "appeal to nature." A good code has a short definition and a clear example, so you (and anyone else) apply it the same way every time. Codes are the buckets you sort your texts into. (Johnny Saldaña's Coding Manual for Qualitative Researchers is the standard guide.)

Concordance (KWIC)

A list of every place a word appears in your corpus, shown with the words on either side, so you can read how it's actually used. KWIC means "keyword in context." Frequency tells you how often a word shows up; a concordance tells you how it's used. Tools like Voyant and AntConc build one in seconds.

Instrument

The research tool you use to collect data from people: a survey, an interview guide, or a set of focus-group questions. In your final report, a copy of your instrument goes in the appendix so readers can see exactly what you asked. It's the difference between "I asked around" and "here is precisely how I measured this."

Closed vs. open questions

Closed questions give set answer choices (multiple choice, a scale), so responses are countable and easy to chart. Open questions let people answer in their own words, richer, but slow to analyze. For P3 you want mostly closed questions (you have to count and visualize) plus a few open ones for quotes and surprises.

Likert scale

The classic agree-to-disagree (or never-to-always) scale, named for psychologist Rensis Likert. Use an odd number of points so there's a genuine neutral middle, label every point (not just the ends), and keep the two sides balanced. It turns an opinion into a number you can average, chart, and compare in Week 12.

Demographic questions

Questions about who the respondent is, age, gender, income, region. They let you compare groups ("do older shoppers trust labels more?"), but they can feel personal, so they go at the end and stay optional. Only ask for a demographic if your analysis will actually use it.

Google Forms

A free survey tool in your Google account. It can collect anonymous responses, supports multiple choice and scales, and drops every answer into a linked Google Sheet you can chart later. For most class projects it's the fastest path from question to countable data.

Primary vs. secondary research

Secondary research means working with what already exists: reading, evaluating, and analyzing sources other people produced. Your literature review and your analysis of food labels were both secondary. Primary research means generating new data yourself, by surveying, interviewing, or observing real people. When you design an instrument and collect responses, you stop being only a reader of research and become an investigator who produces it. Your final report leans on both: secondary work to find the gap, primary work to fill it.

Sample vs. population

Your population is everyone your question is really about (say, "grocery shoppers"). You can't ask all of them, so you ask a sample, a smaller slice. The whole game is choosing a slice that fairly stands in for the whole. A skewed sample answers a narrower question than the one you meant to ask.

Snowball sampling

You ask each participant to pass your survey along to others. It's an easy way to grow a sample and reach people you couldn't find yourself. The catch: friends refer friends, so the snowball tends to stay similar. Nudge it wider by asking people to share with someone unlike them (a different age, a different kind of shopper).

Naming your limitations

Every study has them, and saying so out loud makes you more credible, not less. A small or convenience sample is a limitation, so in your report you write a sentence like, "This sample of 34 mostly-student shoppers can't represent all consumers, so these findings are suggestive, not conclusive." Honest limits are the mark of a real researcher.

Frequency table

A simple count of how many people chose each answer. If 14 of your 42 respondents picked "Sometimes," that's a frequency. Building one turns a column of individual responses into countable totals you can compare and chart, the first step of any quantitative analysis. In Google Sheets, COUNTIF tallies them for you.

Match the chart to the data

Bar chart: comparing separate answer choices (the safe default). Pie chart: parts of one whole that add to 100%, with only a few slices. Line chart: change over time. Grouped or stacked bars: comparing groups against each other. When in doubt, a clean bar chart is rarely wrong. Build yours in the Chart Your Data tool.

Mixed methods

Combining numbers and words so each covers the other's blind spot. Your closed questions give the what (percentages, charts); your open responses and any interviews give the why (quotes, reasons). Setting a participant's own words next to the matching statistic is what makes a quantitative write-up feel human and convincing.

Correlation vs. causation

Two things moving together doesn't mean one caused the other. If label-readers also happen to exercise more, reading labels didn't necessarily cause it, some third thing might drive both. Report what's associated in your data, and don't write that one thing made another happen unless you actually tested it.

IMRAD

The standard structure of a research report: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Nearly every scientific and social-science paper uses it, so readers always know where to look. Your P1 becomes the Introduction, P2 and P3 become Methods and Results, and the Discussion is your new writing that ties it together.

Abstract

A short paragraph (roughly 150 to 250 words) summarizing your whole report: question, method, key finding, and takeaway. It sits near the top, but write it last, once you know what the report actually says. Think of it as the trailer for your study.

Appendix

The back-of-report home for material a reader might want but that would clog the main text: your full survey or interview guide, and your data. Including your instrument is what lets someone judge or repeat your study. Label them "Appendix A," "Appendix B," and refer to them from the Methods.

Figure captions

Every chart or table gets a number and a caption ("Figure 1. How often shoppers look for an organic seal, N = 42"). Captions let a reader understand a visual without hunting through your text, and they make your report read like real research. Number them in the order they appear and mention each in the text.

Reverse outlining

Outlining a draft you've already written to reveal its real structure. Go paragraph by paragraph and note, in one line, the job each does. Lined up together, the outline exposes gaps, repetition, and bad order that finished prose hides. It's the most reliable revision tool there is, because rearranging a skeleton beats rewriting polished paragraphs.

Higher- vs. lower-order concerns

A writing-center framework for revising in the right order. Higher-order concerns: focus, argument, organization, development, the things that decide whether the report works. Lower-order concerns: grammar, spelling, citations, formatting. Fix higher-order first, there's no sense polishing sentences in a paragraph you may cut.

Revision vs. editing

Different jobs. Revision ("re-seeing") works on ideas and structure: what's missing, repeated, out of order, unclear. Editing works on sentences; proofreading catches errors. Do them big to small. Most weak final drafts were edited and proofread but never truly revised.

Reading aloud

Reading your draft out loud (or having your device read it to you) forces you to process every word instead of skimming what you expect to see. It's uncannily good at catching clunky sentences, missing words, and joints where two ideas don't connect. If you stumble reading it, your reader will too. A 2025 study found it caught about three errors per ten-minute paragraph (Eckstein, Lung & Gillette, TESL-EJ).

Informed consent

Before anyone takes part, they get a plain-language explanation of what the study is, what you'll ask, how their data will be used and protected, and that they can stop anytime, and then they freely agree. "Informed" and "voluntary" are the whole idea. No consent, no data.

Anonymous vs. confidential

Not the same thing. Anonymous means you never collect anything that could identify a person, not even you can link a response to a name. Confidential means you might know who said what, but you protect it and never reveal it. If your form asks for a name or email, you can only promise confidentiality.

Vulnerable populations

Groups who may not be able to freely consent, or who could be harmed more easily, and so get extra protection: children, prisoners, people with cognitive impairments, and people under economic, legal, or medical pressure. Farmworkers worried about their jobs and people in recovery can both qualify. Extra care, or a different topic.

IRB (Institutional Review Board)

A committee at a university that reviews research with human participants before it begins, to keep it ethical. The key thing for you: student projects for a class at this level are exempt, so you will not go through IRB review. That's also why some topics are off the table: you can't study people in jails, schoolchildren, or other protected groups, because those need a full review a class project can't do. A short anonymous survey of adults is exactly the kind of thing that's fine.

What we mean by a "social justice lens"

Technical writing is never neutral. Every document, form, chart, or interface makes choices about who can understand it, who it leaves out, and who carries the risk when it fails.

A social justice lens just means we keep asking those questions while we research and write: Who holds power here? Who's served? Whose voice is missing? It's not about scoring political points, it's about noticing that design has real consequences for real people, and doing something about it.
