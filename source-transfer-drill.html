The Source & Claim Transfer Drill

First you catch the weak spots in a stranger's research writing, where it is easy, because you are not attached. Then you turn that same eye on your own claim and evidence. That distance is the whole trick.

This has known answers built in for Part 1, so it can check your diagnosis there. For your own writing in Part 2, it only structures your judgment. It does not read your prose for meaning or grade it. This runs on your computer. Nothing you type leaves it.

Part 1 · Diagnose a stranger's draft

0 of 5 correct. Part 2 unlocks below once all five are right.

"Studies prove organic food is always healthier than conventional food."

Overclaim. "Prove" and "always" promise more than any set of studies delivers, and the evidence on organic nutrition is mixed. Scope it and hedge: "Some studies associate organic produce with lower pesticide residue, though findings on nutrition are mixed."

"Sixty percent of shoppers trust the word 'natural' on a package."

Uncited assertion. A specific number with no source is a red flag. Where did sixty percent come from? Add the citation, or cut the number and make a claim you can actually support.

Labels are barely regulated. "The term 'natural' has no legal definition on most foods." Shoppers deserve better.

Dumped quote. The quotation lands with no naming whose words they are, no citation, and no sentence saying what it means. Frame it: "As the FDA acknowledges, 'the term natural has no legal definition on most foods' (2018), which leaves the word free to imply whatever a marketer wants."

"According to the Organic Trade Association's marketing blog, organic products are the only ethical choice."

Biased or unvetted source. An industry trade group's marketing blog profits from the claim and is not . Use it only as evidence of what the industry says, and corroborate any factual claim with an independent, vetted source.

"It could perhaps be argued that some labels might possibly mislead certain shoppers in some situations."

Over-hedged. So many qualifiers stacked together that the sentence says nothing. Hedging should match a claim to its evidence, not bury it. Commit: "In this sample, three label types were the most likely to be read as health claims."
You just named an overclaim, an uncited assertion, a dumped quote, a biased source, and an over-hedged line, in someone else's writing. Now the real test: the same five live in most research drafts, including yours. Let's look.

That is the drill: the weak spots are always easier to see in someone else's writing. Practicing on a stranger trains the eye you then turn on your own.

Signal phrase

A few words that name whose voice you are quoting and frame their point before the quotation lands: "Smith argues that…," "According to the FDA…." It hands the reader the source's identity and your take in one move, so a quote never appears out of nowhere.

Peer-reviewed

Vetted by other experts in the field before publication. Not a guarantee of truth, but a real filter. A trade group's marketing blog, a press release, or a company's own "research" page has not been through that filter, so it needs corroboration before you lean on it, especially when the source profits from the claim.
